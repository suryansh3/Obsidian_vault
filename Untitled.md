### Section 1: Types of Writing and Audience Analysis

This is a fundamental concept tested in various forms.

**Key Questions:** 1(a), 1(b), 2(a) from 2023; 2 from 2024.

**Core Concepts to Remember:**
*   **Creative Writing:** Focus on artistic expression, imagination, and entertainment. (Characteristics: artistic freedom, explores themes, uses narrative techniques).
*   **Scientific Writing:** Focus on factual accuracy, clarity, and objectivity. It describes research, experiments, and findings.
*   **Technical Writing:** Focus on instructing, informing, or persuading a specific audience to perform a task or understand a process. It is user-centered and simplifies complex information.
    *   **Examples from Paper:** A user guide, an IKEA manual, a nutrition label.

**Audience Types (The "Mixed Audience"):**
*   **Primary:** The main users (e.g., customers using the smartphone).
*   **Secondary:** Those who might be consulted (e.g., tech experts checking for errors).
*   **Tertiary/Gatekeepers:** Those who have a stake in the document's success (e.g., executives, lawyers).

**How to Answer/Recall:**
*   When asked "Is X creative/scientific/technical?", first define the type of writing.
*   Then, match the characteristics of the given example (e.g., an autobiography has facts but also storytelling) to your definition to justify your "Yes" or "No" answer.
*   For audience analysis, always think: **Who uses this? Who checks it? Who pays for it?**

### Section 2: Language Mechanics and Style

This section tests your command over the English language in a formal context.

**Key Questions:** 2(a), 2(b) from 2023; 1(b), 1(d), 1(h), 3(b) from 2024.

**Core Concepts to Remember:**
*   **Italics:** Used for *emphasis*, *foreign words*, *titles of major works* (books, films), and *scientific names* (e.g., *E. coli*).
*   **Active vs. Passive Voice:**
    *   **Passive:** The experiment **was conducted**. (The "doer" is hidden).
    *   **Active:** The scientists **conducted** the experiment. (Clear, direct, and preferred).
*   **Nominalizations:** Turning verbs into bulky nouns. Avoid them.
    *   *Conduct an investigation* → *Investigate*
    *   *Make a comparison* → *Compare*
*   **Punctuation:** Semicolons (`;`) can link two closely related independent clauses.
*   **Transitions:** Words that create flow (e.g., *however, therefore, since, for example*).

**How to Answer/Recall:**
*   For "correct this sentence" questions, read the sentence aloud in your mind. Often, awkward phrasing will reveal the error.
*   Remember the **"Zombie Test"** for active voice: If you can add "by zombies" after the verb and it makes sense, it's passive. (e.g., "The experiment was conducted *by zombies*.") Rewrite it to be active.
*   Look for words ending in *-tion, -ment, -ance*. They are often nominalizations that can be simplified.

### Section 3: Document Structure and Organization

This is about the "skeleton" of academic and technical documents.

**Key Questions:** 3(b), 4(a) from 2023; 1(e), 1(f), 4(a) from 2024.

**Core Concepts to Remember:**
*   **Thesis Structure (Sequence is KEY):**
    1.  **Prefatory Pages (Front Matter):** Cover Page, Approval Certificates, Declaration, Acknowledgements, Abstract, Table of Contents, List of Figures/Tables. (Use Roman numerals: i, ii, iii...).
    2.  **Chapters (Main Body):** Introduction, Literature Review, Methodology, etc. (Use cardinal numbers: 1, 2, 3...).
    3.  **References & Appendices (Back Matter):** (Use cardinal numbers).
*   **Research Manuscript Sections:** Title → Abstract → Introduction → Literature Review → Methodology → Results → Discussion → Conclusion → References.
*   **Abstract:** A dense summary of the **Problem, Method, Key Results, and Conclusion**.
*   **Table of Contents vs. List of Figures/Tables:** The ToC lists chapters/sections. The LoF/LoT lists only the figures and tables with their page numbers.

**How to Answer/Recall:**
*   **Mnemonic for Manuscript Order:** **T**itle → **A**bstract → **I**ntroduce → **L**iterature → **M**ethodology → **R**esults → **D**iscuss → **C**onclude → **R**eferences. (**TAIL MR DCR**).
*   Practice drawing a quick flowchart of a thesis's front matter from memory.

### Section 4: Research Ethics and Referencing

This is about the integrity of your work.

**Key Questions:** 2(a) from 2023 (OR part); 4(b), 5(a) from 2023; 5(a) from 2024.

**Core Concepts to Remember:**
*   **Plagiarism:** Using others' work without credit.
*   **Self-Plagiarism:** Reusing your own previously published work without citation, as if it were new.
*   **Steps to Avoid Plagiarism:** **P-C-D**
    1.  **Paraphrase** properly (restate in your own words).
    2.  Always **Cite** the original source.
    3.  Use **Direct quotes** sparingly and with quotation marks.
*   **Referencing Styles:**
    *   **Author-Date (APA, Harvard):** (Smith, 2020) in text.
    *   **Author-Number (Vancouver):** A number in brackets [1] in text.
    *   **Footnote Style:** A superscript number with the citation at the bottom of the page.
*   **Key Reference Elements:**
    *   **Book:** Author, (Year). *Title*. Publisher.
    *   **Journal Article:** Author, (Year). Title. *Journal Name, Volume*(Issue), Page range.
    *   **Website:** Author/Organization, (Year, Month Day). *Title of Page*. Site Name. URL

**How to Answer/Recall:**
*   Remember that **self-plagiarism is still plagiarism**. It misleads the reader about the novelty of your work.
*   For referencing, learn **one style (like APA) thoroughly**. You can then adapt the logic to others. The key is **consistency**.

### Section 5: Data Representation and Visual Communication

Turning numbers into understandable information.

**Key Questions:** 4(b), 5(a) from 2023; 5(b) from 2024.

**Core Concepts to Remember:**
*   **Tables:** For presenting **exact values** and comparing multiple parameters. Label as **Table 1: Descriptive Caption Above**.
*   **Graphs/Figures (Bar, Line):** For showing **trends, patterns, and comparisons**. Label as **Fig. 1: Descriptive Caption Below**.
*   **Essential Graph/Table Elements:**
    *   Clear Title/Caption
    *   Labeled X and Y axes with units
    *   A legend if multiple data series are present
    *   Correct scaling

**How to Answer/Recall:**
*   Ask yourself: "Do I need the precise number (use a table) or the overall trend (use a graph)?"
*   When drawing a graph in an exam, use a ruler for neatness. Clearly mark the axes and data points.

### Section 6: Process Description and Flowcharts

Showing a sequence of steps logically.

**Key Questions:** 5(b) from 2023; 6(b) from 2024.

**Core Concepts to Remember:**
*   A flowchart uses standardized shapes:
    *   **Oval:** Start/End
    *   **Rectangle:** Process/Step
    *   **Diamond:** Decision (Yes/No question)
    *   **Parallelogram:** Input/Output
*   The logic must be clear, with decision points leading to unambiguous paths.

**How to Answer/Recall:**
*   Before drawing, list the steps and identify all the **decision points** (e.g., "Is the date valid?", "Did they qualify?").
*   Trace your finished flowchart to ensure there are no dead ends and that every path leads to a logical conclusion.

### Final Exam Strategy

1.  **Read Questions Carefully:** Underline key action words like "mention," "explain," "justify," "rewrite."
2.  **Manage Time:** Allocate time based on marks. A 5-mark question deserves more time than a 1-mark question.
3.  **Structure Your Answers:**
    *   For definitions, write a clear, concise sentence.
    *   For explanations, use the "**Point + Explain + Example**" structure.
    *   For "list and explain," use bullet points for the list and a short sentence for each explanation.
4.  **Be Neat:** Especially for flowcharts, graphs, and tables. A clear presentation makes a good impression.
