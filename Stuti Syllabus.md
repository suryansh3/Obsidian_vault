### BLOCK 1: INTRODUCTION TO ECONOMIC GROWTH

#### UNIT 1: Economic Growth - Concept and Measurement

**Definition**: Economic growth refers to the increase in the inflation-adjusted market value of the goods and services produced by an economy over time. It is conventionally measured as the percent rate of increase in real gross domestic product (GDP).

**Key Concepts:**
- **Growth vs Development**: Growth is a quantitative increase in output; development is a broader concept encompassing improvements in living standards, health, education, and reduction in poverty.
- **Measurement**: GDP growth rate, GNP, per capita income, PPP-adjusted measures
- **Kaldor's Stylized Facts** (for reference):
  1. Output per worker grows at a roughly constant rate
  2. Capital per worker grows at a roughly constant rate
  3. Capital-output ratio is roughly constant
  4. Rate of profit on capital is roughly constant
  5. Share of wages and profits in national income is roughly constant

---

#### UNIT 2: Harrod-Domar Growth Model

**Structure of the Model**:

The model expresses economic growth (g) as:
**g = s / v**

Where:
- **g** = Growth rate of output
- **s** = Savings rate (S/Y)
- **v** = Capital-output ratio (K/Y)

**Key Assumptions**:
1. Fixed capital-output ratio (constant v)
2. All savings are automatically converted into investment
3. No government intervention
4. Closed economy with no foreign trade
5. Labour is homogeneous
6. Constant returns to scale
7. No technological progress
8. Fixed proportion of labour and capital in production

**Three Growth Rates**:
1. **Actual Growth Rate (G)**: Actual rate of output growth determined by savings and capital-output ratio
2. **Warranted Growth Rate (Gw)**: Growth rate required to maintain full capacity utilization (Gw = s/v)
3. **Natural Growth Rate (Gn)**: Growth rate determined by population growth and technological progress

**Instability Problem - "Knife-Edge" Equilibrium**:
- If G > Gw: Actual growth exceeds warranted → excess demand → inflationary pressures
- If G < Gw: Actual growth falls short → excess capacity → recession
- The economy rarely stays on the warranted growth path

**Limitations**:
1. **Knife-edge instability**: No automatic mechanism ensuring G = Gw
2. **Fixed coefficients assumption**: Unrealistic - factor substitution is possible
3. **Ignores institutional framework**: Infrastructure, financial markets, bureaucracy
4. **No role for prices**: Prices don't adjust to clear factor markets
5. **Capital fundamentalism**: Overemphasis on physical capital accumulation
6. **No technological change**: Treats technology as exogenous

**Practice Question**: *Discuss the structure of the Harrod-Domar model. Describe its main assumptions. What are its limitations?* (Asked in Jun'24 Q1)

#### UNIT 3: Solow (Neo-classical) Growth Model

**Structure of the Model**:

Production function: **Y = F(K, L)** with constant returns to scale

In per-worker terms: **y = f(k)** where y = Y/L, k = K/L

**Key Equation**: **Δk = sf(k) - (n + δ)k**

Where:
- s = savings rate
- n = population growth rate
- δ = depreciation rate
- k = capital per worker

**Steady State**: Δk = 0 → **sf(k*) = (n + δ)k***

**How Solow Model Accounts for Long-Run Growth**:

1. **Capital accumulation** alone cannot sustain long-run growth due to diminishing returns to capital
2. In the **steady state**, output per worker grows at rate **zero** (in the basic model)
3. **Technological progress** is the only engine of long-term growth
4. Technological progress is **exogenous** in this model

**Predictions of the Model**:
- Countries with higher savings/investment rates → higher levels of capital and income per worker
- Countries with higher population growth → lower levels of capital and income per worker
- Convergence: Poor countries grow faster than rich countries (conditional convergence)

**Difference from Harrod-Domar Model**:
| Feature | Harrod-Domar | Solow |
|---------|--------------|-------|
| Capital-output ratio | Fixed | Variable |
| Factor substitution | Not possible | Possible |
| Stability | Knife-edge | Stable steady state |
| Role of prices | None | Prices adjust |
| Long-run growth | Determined by s/v | Determined by technology |

**Practice Question**: *Explain how the Solow model accounts for long-run economic growth. Describe its structure and working.* (Asked in Jun'24 Q2)

#### UNIT 4: Kaldor's Growth Model

**Key Features**:
- Cambridge (Keynesian) growth model
- Savings propensities attached to **income categories** (profits and wages)
- Focus on distribution of income between wages and profits
- Investment determines savings (post-Keynesian)

**Kaldor's Model**:

**Savings Function**: S = s_w W + s_p P
Where:
- s_w = propensity to save out of wages (low)
- s_p = propensity to save out of profits (high)

**Equilibrium Condition**: I = S

**Income Distribution**: P/Y = (1/(s_p - s_w)) × (I/Y) - (s_w/(s_p - s_w))

**Technical Progress Function**: Links productivity growth to investment

**Pasinetti's Theory - Difference from Kaldor**:

| Aspect | Kaldor | Pasinetti |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| Savings propensities | Attached to income categories (wages/profits) | Attached to social classes (workers/capitalists) |
| Workers' savings | Not considered as capital owners | Workers save and become owners of capital |
| Focus | Macroeconomic distribution | Logical consistency of distribution |
| Approach | Behavioural | Technical/logical |

Pasinetti's model is a **refinement/correction** of Kaldor's model.

**Practice Question**: *Discuss Kaldor's model of economic growth. In what way is Pasinetti's theory of economic growth different from that of Kaldor?* (Asked in Jun'24 Q3)

#### UNIT 4 (Extended): Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans Model

**Ramsey Model**:
- Optimizing growth model with **intertemporal utility maximization**
- Households choose consumption/savings to maximize lifetime utility
- Social planner chooses capital and consumption paths

**Euler Equation (Keynes-Ramsey Rule)**:
```
ċ/c = (1/θ)(MPK - ρ - δ)
```
Where:
- θ = coefficient of relative risk aversion
- MPK = marginal product of capital
- ρ = rate of time preference
- δ = depreciation rate

**Modified Golden Rule**:
- Level of capital per worker that **maximizes consumption**
- Condition: **f'(k*) = n + δ + θg**
- Allows for dynamic efficiency

**Cass-Koopmans Extension of Ramsey Model**:

| Feature | Ramsey Model | Cass-Koopmans Model |
|---------|--------------|---------------------|
| Population growth | Constant | Endogenous/exogenous |
| Technological progress | Not included | Included |
| Dynamics | Basic optimization | More complex dynamics |
| Steady state | Unique | Multiple equilibria possible |

**Practice Question**: *Analyse the Ramsey model of economic growth. Explain the 'Golden Rule of Capital Accumulation'. In what way does the Cass-Koopman's model extend the Ramsey model?* (Asked in Jun'24 Q4)

### BLOCK 2: TECHNOLOGY, PRODUCTIVITY AND GROWTH

#### UNIT 5: Technical Change and Economic Growth

**Hicks' Classification of Technical Change**:

Compares points on different per-worker output curves at **points of constant capital-labour ratio**.

1. **Hicks-Neutral** (Labour-saving): MPK/MPL ratio constant at same K/L
2. **Labour-saving** (Capital-using): MPK/MPL increases at same K/L
3. **Capital-saving** (Labour-using): MPK/MPL decreases at same K/L

**Harrod's Classification of Technical Change**:

Compares points on different per-worker output curves at **points of constant capital-output ratio**.

1. **Harrod-Neutral** (Labour-augmenting): Capital-output ratio constant at constant interest rate
2. **Labour-saving**: Capital-output ratio increases
3. **Capital-saving**: Capital-output ratio decreases

**Key Difference**:
- **Hicks**: Compares at constant K/L ratio
- **Harrod**: Compares at constant K/Y ratio

**Practice Question**: *Discuss Hicks's classification of technical change.* (Asked in Jun'24 Q5)

#### UNIT 6: Total Factor Productivity (TFP) and Growth Accounting

**Growth Accounting Framework**:

ΔY/Y = ΔA/A + α(ΔK/K) + (1-α)(ΔL/L)

Where:
- ΔY/Y = Growth rate of output
- ΔA/A = TFP growth (Solow residual)
- α = Capital's share of income
- (1-α) = Labour's share of income

**Solow Residual**: The part of output growth not explained by growth in inputs

**Measurement Methods for TFP**:

1. **Index Method**:
   - Uses index numbers (Laspeyres, Paasche, Tornqvist)
   - Tornqvist index is most commonly used
   - Requires data on input quantities and prices

2. **Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA)**:
   - Non-parametric method
   - Uses linear programming
   - Compares efficiency of decision-making units
   - No need for specifying production function form

#### UNIT 10: Growth Models under Uncertainty - Brock-Mirman Model

**Brock-Mirman Model**:
- Stochastic version of the optimal growth model
- Incorporates **productivity shocks**
- Key for understanding **Real Business Cycle (RBC)** models

**How it helps understand uncertainty in RBC models**:
1. Introduces **stochastic productivity** in the production function
2. Households maximize expected utility: E[ΣβᵗU(cₜ)]
3. Firms face productivity shocks (technology shocks)
4. Explains business cycle fluctuations as optimal responses to shocks
5. Shows how uncertainty affects investment and consumption decisions

#### UNIT 11-12: Endogenous Growth Models

**Salient Features of Endogenous Growth Models**:
1. **Growth is endogenous** - determined within the model, not by exogenous technology
2. **No diminishing returns** to capital (in aggregate)
3. **Knowledge spillovers** and externalities
4. **Human capital** as a key driver
5. **Increasing returns** to scale at aggregate level
6. **Policy matters** - taxes, subsidies affect growth

**Lucas Model (Human Capital-Induced Growth)**:

- **Two sectors**: Goods production and education sector
- **Human capital** enters production function
- Individuals allocate time between work and human capital accumulation

**Key Equations**:
- Production: Y = AKᵅ(uH)¹⁻ᵅ
- Human capital accumulation: Ḣ = δ(1-u)H

Where:
- u = fraction of time spent working
- (1-u) = fraction of time spent on education
- H = stock of human capital

**Implications**:
- Constant returns to reproducible factors → no diminishing returns
- Growth depends on time allocated to education
- Human capital externalities generate sustained growth

**Romer Model (Knowledge Spillovers)**:
- **Knowledge** as a non-rival, partially excludable good
- **Monopolistic competition** in intermediate goods
- **Research sector** creates new ideas (blueprints)
- Spillovers from knowledge → increasing returns

**AK Model**:
- Production: Y = AK (linear in capital)
- No diminishing returns to capital
- Growth rate: g = sA - (n+δ)
- **Critique**: Doesn't explain why returns don't diminish

### BLOCK 4: DEVELOPMENT AND UNDERDEVELOPMENT

#### UNIT 15: Kuznets' Inverted-U Hypothesis

**The Hypothesis**:
- As economic development proceeds, **inequality first increases and then decreases** - forming an "inverted-U" shape
- Transition from agriculture to industry: Initial increase in inequality
- Later, as more workers move to high-productivity sectors, inequality falls

**Explanation**:
1. **Early stage**: Rural-urban migration, industrial sector growth → wage differentials widen
2. **Middle stage**: Inequality peaks
3. **Late stage**: Universal education, welfare states, skilled labour abundance → inequality falls

**Relevance Today**:

| Supporting Evidence | Contradicting Evidence |
|---------------------|----------------------|
| Some countries: Brazil, South Korea | Many developed countries show rising inequality |
| Cross-country studies show inverted-U | Inequality has increased in US, UK |
| | Environmental Kuznets Curve is more accepted |

**Critique**:
- Not a universal law
- Depends on institutional factors
- Political economy matters

**Practice Question**: *Discuss Kuznets' 'inverted-U' hypothesis. Discuss its relevance today.* (Asked in Jun'24 Q8)

#### UNIT 16: Labour Migration - Harris-Todaro Model

**Background**:
- Developed by Harris and Todaro (1970)
- Explains rural-urban migration in developing countries

**Key Features**:
- Migration depends on **expected** wage differentials, not actual
- Rural-urban migration continues even with urban unemployment

**The Model**:

**Expected Urban Wage** = Actual Urban Wage × Probability of Employment

**Equilibrium Condition**:
Rural Wage = Expected Urban Wage

**Mathematical**:
- Let wᵤ = urban wage, wᵣ = rural wage
- Let Lᵤ = urban employment, Lᵤᵤ = urban unemployed
- Probability of employment = Lᵤ/(Lᵤ + Lᵤᵤ)
- Equilibrium: wᵣ = wᵤ × Lᵤ/(Lᵤ + Lᵤᵤ)

**Implications**:
- Urban unemployment is a rational outcome
- Policy should focus on rural development
- Urban job creation alone may worsen unemployment (by attracting more migrants)

**Relevance for Developing Nations**:
1. Explains persistent urban unemployment in developing countries
2. Highlights importance of rural-urban wage gap
3. Policy implications: Address rural-urban imbalance
4. Still relevant for understanding migration patterns

### BLOCK 6: STATE, MARKETS AND INSTITUTIONS

#### UNIT 24: Role of the State in Development

**State Functions**:
1. **Provider**: Public goods (defence, infrastructure, education)
2. **Regulator**: Market regulation, anti-trust, consumer protection
3. **Redistributor**: Progressive taxation, welfare programmes
4. **Stabilizer**: Macroeconomic management
5. **Developer**: Industrial policy, R&D support

**Government Failure vs Market Failure**【Jun'25 Q7】:

| Market Failure | Government Failure |
|----------------|-------------------|
| Externalities | Bureaucratic inefficiency |
| Public goods | Rent-seeking |
| Monopoly power | Regulatory capture |
| Information asymmetry | Political interference |
| Inequality | Fiscal deficits |

**Distinction**: Government failure is NOT the same as market failure. Government failure occurs when:
1. **Regulatory capture**: Regulators serve industry interests
2. **Bureaucratic inefficiency**: Red tape, delays
3. **Information problems**: Governments have limited information
4. **Political economy**: Short-term political considerations

### 4.1 Limitations of Per Capita Income as Welfare Indicator【Jun'24 Q10】

1. **Distribution**: Doesn't account for income inequality
2. **Non-market activities**: Ignores household production, volunteer work
3. **Environmental costs**: Doesn't deduct environmental degradation
4. **Quality of life**: Doesn't measure health, education, happiness
5. **Informal economy**: Excludes unrecorded economic activity
6. **Purchasing power**: Doesn't account for cost of living differences
7. **Negative externalities**: Crime, pollution reduce welfare but not captured
8. **Leisure**: Ignores value of leisure time

### 4.2 Global Supply Chain vs Global Value Chain【Jun'24 Q11】

| Global Supply Chain (GSC) | Global Value Chain (GVC) |
|---------------------------|--------------------------|
| Focus on **logistics and flow** of goods | Focus on **value addition** at each stage |
| Physical movement of materials | Distribution of profits and value |
| Operational perspective | Strategic perspective |
| Efficiency and cost minimization | Competitive advantage |
| Supplier relationships | Governance and upgrading |

**Demographic Dividend**:

- Opportunity for growth if human capital is developed

**Challenges**:
- Employment creation for millions of youth
- Skill development
- Gender gap in labour force participation
- Aging population in the future

### 4.4 Behavioural Development Economics【Jun'25 Q8】

**Difference from Traditional Development Economics**:

| Traditional | Behavioural |
|-------------|-------------|
| Rational agents | Bounded rationality |
| Self-interest | Social preferences |
| Complete information | Information processing biases |
| Standard utility maximization | Heuristics and biases |
| Policy: incentives | Policy: nudges and choice architecture |

**Applications**:
- Savings behaviour (commitment devices)
- Health decisions (present bias)
- Educational choices (overconfidence)
- Technology adoption (social learning)

### 4.5 Schumpeter's Theory of Capitalist Development【Jun'25 Q5】

**Key Concepts**:
- **Innovation** as the engine of growth
- **Creative destruction**: New innovations destroy old industries
- **Entrepreneur** as the innovator
- Five types of innovation: new products, new processes, new markets, new sources of supply, new organization

### 4.6 Hysteresis【Jun'25 Q10】

**Definition**: The dependence of a system's state on its history.

**Economic Consequences**:
- **Unemployment persistence**: Recessions can permanently raise unemployment
- **Trade**: Once lost, markets may not be regained
- **Institutions**: Past decisions constrain future options (path dependence)
- **Human capital**: Skills can become obsolete permanently
- **Firm dynamics**: Business failures during recessions may be permanent

### 4.7 Path Dependence Theory【Dec'25 Q8】

**Definition**: History matters for economic outcomes; past decisions constrain future possibilities.

**Key Points**:
1. **Increasing returns** to adoption of technologies/institutions
2. **Switching costs** make change expensive
3. **Network effects** create lock-in
4. **Example**: QWERTY keyboard vs. more efficient alternatives
5. **Institutional path dependence**: Colonial institutions persist

### 4.8 Geography and Economic Development【Dec'25 Q5】

**How Geography Affects Development**:

1. **Natural resources**: Endowment of minerals, oil, fertile land
2. **Climate**: Effects on agriculture, disease burden
3. **Coastal access**: Trade opportunities
4. **Transport costs**: Landlocked countries at disadvantage
5. **Disease environment**: Tropical diseases reduce productivity

**Critique**:
- Institutions mediate geographical effects
- Technology can overcome geographical constraints
- Historical contingency matters

### 4.9 Income Inequality and Economic Growth【Dec'25 Q6】

**Introduction** (2 marks)

- First attempt to explain growth in a formal model
- Post-Keynesian framework

**Structure** (5 marks)
- g = s/v
- Actual, Warranted, Natural growth rates
- Capital fundamentalism

**Assumptions** (5 marks)
- Fixed capital-output ratio
- Full employment of savings
- No government, no foreign trade
- Constant returns, no technical change

**Limitations** (6 marks)
- Knife-edge instability
- No factor substitution
- Ignores institutional factors
- No role for prices
- Oversimplification

**Conclusion** (2 marks)
- Important foundation for growth theory despite limitations
- Influenced development policy in 1950s-60s
</details>

<summary><b>Q2: Solow Model - Structure, Working, Long-run Growth</b></summary>

**Introduction** (2 marks)
- Solow (1956), Neo-classical growth model
- Nobel Prize-winning contribution

**Structure** (6 marks)
- Production function: Y = F(K,L)
- Per-worker: y = f(k)
- Capital accumulation: Δk = sf(k) - (n+δ)k

**Working** (5 marks)
- Steady state determination
- Transition dynamics
- Convergence

**Long-run Growth** (5 marks)
- Capital accumulation alone cannot sustain growth
- Technological progress is the engine
- Exogenous technology assumption

**Conclusion** (2 marks)
- Foundation for modern growth theory
- Policy implications: savings, population, technology
</details>

**Introduction** (2 marks)
- Harris and Todaro (1970)
- Explains rural-urban migration in developing countries

**Model Explanation** (5 marks)
- Migration based on expected wages
- Equilibrium: Rural wage = Expected urban wage
- Urban unemployment persists

**Relevance** (4 marks)
- Explains paradox of urban unemployment despite high wages
- Policy implications for developing countries
- Still relevant today (China, India)

**Conclusion** (1 mark)
- Important contribution to development economics

- Human capital accumulation
