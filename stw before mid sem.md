### **1. Core Concepts & Ethics**

#### **A. Plagiarism (The Cardinal Sin)**

| **Concept** | **Key Point** |
| :--- | :--- |
| **What is it?** | Using someone's work/ideas without credit, for gain, in a situation expecting originality. |
| **Plagiarism vs. Copyright** | **Plagiarism:** Ethical offense (about credit). **Copyright:** Legal offense (about permission). They overlap but aren't the same. |
| **Self-Plagiarism** | Reusing your own previous work without citation. Also called "recycling fraud." |
| **AI & Plagiarism** | Using AI-generated content without authorization is considered plagiarism (e.g., "high-tech plagiarism"). Always check your institution's policy. |

**🔍 10 Common Forms of Plagiarism (Turnitin) - Flashcard Style**
> **Front:** Submitting someone's work as your own.
> **Back:** **Clone Plagiarism.** The most direct form.
> ---
> **Front:** Taking text from your own previous work.
> **Back:** **Self-Plagiarism.** Must cite your old papers!
> ---
> **Front:** Re-writing someone's work without citing.
> **Back:** **Paraphrasing Plagiarism.** Changing words isn't enough.
> ---
> **Front:** Using quotes but not citing the source.
> **Back:** **"Forgotten Citation."** The source is missing.
> ---
> **Front:** Mixing sources without citations.
> **Back:** **"Mashup" or "Patchwork" Plagiarism.**
> ---
> **Front:** Citing some, but not all, copied passages.
> **Back:** **"Half-Cite."** Incomplete citation.
> ---
> **Front:** Mixing cited and uncited sections.
> **Back:** **"Hybrid" or "Mix & Match" Plagiarism.**
> ---
> **Front:** Proper citation, but the text is too similar.
> **Back:** **"Poor Paraphrase."** Structure/wording is copied.
> ---
> **Front:** Citing a source incorrectly.
> **Back:** **"Invalid Source."** Misleading citation.
> ---
> **Front:** Relying too much on others' work.
> **Back:** **"Lazy Research."** Lacks original thought.

#### **B. Bias-Free Language (Writing with Respect)**

| **Area** | **Avoid (Biased)** | **Use (Bias-Free)** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Gender** | Chairman, mankind, fireman | Chairperson, humanity, firefighter |
| **Gender (Pronouns)** | Each student must submit **his** work. | All students must submit **their** work. |
| **Disability** | Confined to a wheelchair, suffers from | Uses a wheelchair, diagnosed with |
| **Disability (Language)** | "Lame idea," "blind luck" | "Weak idea," "pure luck" |
| **Race/Ethnicity** | Minority, Oriental | Historically marginalized, Asian (be specific: e.g., Korean) |
| **Best Practice** | The mentally ill, the poor | People with mental illness, people with low income |

**💡 Golden Rule:** Use **People-First Language**. Put the person before the condition (e.g., "person with a disability," not "disabled person").

### **2. Writing Mechanics & Grammar**

#### **A. Voice & Nominalization**

| **Concept** | **When to Use** | **Example** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Active Voice** | Default. Emphasizes the doer. Clear and direct. | **We analyzed** the data. |
| **Passive Voice** | Emphasizes the action/receiver. Doer is unknown/unimportant. Common in Methods sections. | The data **was analyzed**. |
| **Nominalization** | Converting verbs to nouns. Makes writing more formal and concise for tech/scientific writing. | **Verb:** We evaluated the results. <br> **Noun:** The **evaluation** of the results... |

#### **B. Common Errors**

| **Error Type** | **What it is** | **Incorrect** | **Correct** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Dangling Modifier** | A modifier with no logical subject in the sentence. | *While coding, the computer crashed.* | *While I was coding, the computer crashed.* |
| **Faulty Parallelism** | Mixing structures in a list. | She likes **swimming**, **to bike**, and **running**. | She likes **swimming**, **biking**, and **running**. |
| **Informal Writing** | Using colloquial language in formal docs. | The results were, **you know**, kinda weird. | The results were anomalous. |

#### **C. Punctuation Quick Guide**

*   **Comma (,):** After intro clauses, in lists, before FANBOYS (For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So) connecting independent clauses.
*   **Semicolon (;):** Connects two closely related independent clauses (without a conjunction).
*   **Colon (:):** Introduces a list or explanation. Don't use after "such as" or "including."
*   **Dash (—):** For strong emphasis or parenthetical info. Use sparingly in tech writing.

### **3. Structuring Your Writing**

#### **A. Patterns of Idea Development**

| **Pattern** | **Purpose** | **Example Topic** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Narration** | To tell a story or sequence events. | The history of the internet. |
| **Description** | To vividly present a person, place, or thing. | The design of the new quantum processor. |
| **Example** | To illustrate a general idea with specifics. | Carnivorous plants, **such as** the Venus flytrap... |
| **Definition** | To explain what something is. | What is blockchain? |
| **Comparison/Contrast** | To examine similarities and/or differences. | Compare iOS and Android operating systems. |
| **Cause & Effect** | To explain why something happened. | The effects of social media on public discourse. |
| **Problem & Solution** | To identify a problem and propose a fix. | Addressing the issue of plastic waste in oceans. |
| **Process Analysis** | To explain how to do something (step-by-step). | How to assemble a PC. |
| **Classification** | To break a topic into parts or types. | Types of machine learning algorithms. |

#### **B. Transitions & Signposts (The Glue)**

**Use these words to guide your reader:**

| **Function** | **Examples** |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Adding/Sequencing** | Furthermore, Moreover, Subsequently, Finally, First...Second...Third... |
| **Contrasting** | However, Conversely, On the other hand, Alternatively, Despite this |
| **Giving Example** | For instance, Namely, Specifically, To illustrate |
| **Showing Cause/Effect** | Consequently, Therefore, As a result, Hence, Accordingly |
| **Summarizing** | In conclusion, To summarize, In short, Ultimately |

**📌 Signposting Phrases:**
*   **Major Signpost:** "This essay will argue that..."
*   **Transition Sentence:** "Having discussed X, it is now necessary to consider Y."
*   **Reminder:** "As mentioned in the previous section..."

### **4. Audience Analysis & Document Types**

#### **A. Know Your Audience**

| **Audience Type** | **Who They Are** | **What They Need** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Experts** | Designers, researchers. Deep technical knowledge. | Jargon, high detail, peer-review level depth. |
| **Technicians** | Build, operate, repair. Hands-on knowledge. | Practical steps, specifications, maintenance info. |
| **Executives** | Decision-makers (CEOs, managers). Little technical knowledge. | The "big picture," costs, benefits, bottom line. |
| **Gatekeepers** | Editors, legal, compliance. | Documents that follow rules and standards. |
| **Non-Specialists** | General public, end-users. Least technical knowledge. | Simple language, no jargon, "how-to" focus. |

**Always identify your Primary Audience** and tailor the document's content, language, and depth to them.

#### **B. Types of Writing - At a Glance**

| **Writing Type** | **Audience** | **Purpose** | **Key Feature** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Scientific Writing** | Other experts (peers). | Record and share research (IMRaD). | Highly technical, objective, detailed. |
| **Science Writing** | General public (non-specialists). | Explain science in an accessible way. | Avoids jargon, uses analogies. |
| **Technical Writing** | Varied (users, technicians, execs). | Explain, instruct, or describe. | Clarity and usability are paramount. |
| **Business Writing** | Internal/External stakeholders. | Inform, persuade, transact business. | Professional, goal-oriented. |
| **Creative Writing** | General public. | Express, entertain, engage. | Artistic freedom, narrative, style. |
| **Content Writing** | Online users, customers. | Market, brand, and sell. | SEO-friendly, engaging, brand-aligned. |

### **5. Formatting & Professional Touches**

#### **A. Lists (A Key Tech Writing Tool)**

| **List Type** | **Use When...** | **Formatting Tip** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bulleted List** | Items have no specific order. | Use hanging indent. Punctuate only if items are complete sentences. |
| **Numbered List** | Order is important (e.g., steps). | Use for instructions or sequences. |
| **In-Sentence List** | List is short and fits smoothly in a sentence. | Use (a), (b), (c) and commas/semicolons. |
| **Two-Column List** | For pairs (e.g., terms & definitions). | Align left; it's essentially a table with invisible lines. |

| **Abbr.** | **Latin** | **Meaning** | **Use** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **e.g.** | exempli gratia | for example | Introduces examples. |
| **i.e.** | id est | that is | Clarifies or rephrases. |
| **etc.** | et cetera | and so on | Ends a non-exhaustive list. |

### **📝 Final Cheat Sheet: The 7 C's of Effective Technical Writing**

1.  **Clarity:** Be unambiguous and easy to understand.
2.  **Conciseness:** Be brief and to the point. Omit needless words.
3.  **Concreteness:** Use specific facts, figures, and examples.
4.  **Correctness:** Use accurate information and proper grammar.
5.  **Coherence:** Ensure ideas flow logically (use transitions!).
6.  **Completeness:** Provide all necessary information.
7.  **Courtesy:** Be respectful and bias-free.
