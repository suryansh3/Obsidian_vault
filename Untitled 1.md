## 1. Problem Statement

Speech is produced by a source–filter mechanism: a quasi-periodic glottal excitation is shaped by the resonances of the vocal tract, so the information-bearing cues of a word lie in a slowly varying spectral envelope, not in the waveform samples. That envelope changes within tens of milliseconds, so the signal is non-stationary, and both glottal pitch and vocal-tract shape differ from speaker to speaker, so the same word gives very different sample sequences for different talkers. A recogniser must therefore convert each utterance into a compact, loudness- and pitch-insensitive descriptor and compare utterances of unequal duration. This assignment designs, implements and measures such a pipeline with pre-mid-semester techniques only, settling five sub-problems:

- (P1) Short-time analysis – choose frame length, window and hop that make short-time spectral estimation valid, and fix the DFT size.
    
- (P2) Feature design – obtain a low-dimensional envelope descriptor robust to speaker variation and compare MFCC, LPCC and a plain DFT log-spectrum under identical conditions.
    
- (P3) Parametric modelling – fit an all-pole LPC model per frame, recover formants by root solving and quantify their accuracy.
    
- (P4) Classification – label an unknown utterance of arbitrary length from a few examples per word with no trained model.
    
- (P5) Evaluation – measure accuracy in matched and mismatched conditions and state the advantages and limits of each front end.
    

## 2. Solution Given

Five stages: a source–filter synthesiser that generates the vocabulary and keeps the true formants so feature error is measurable; conditioning and framing; three parallel feature extractors; an LPC root-based formant estimator; and a dynamic-time-warping (DTW) classifier evaluated leave-one-speaker-out.

**Test material.** Five isolated words /a/, /i/, /u/, /ai/, /ia/ at Fs = 8 kHz, generated by a cascade of three second-order resonators (F1–F3, bandwidths 60–120 Hz) driven by an impulse train — the source–filter model of Module I. Four simulated speakers used F0 = 100, 120, 150, 180 Hz and vocal-tract scaling 0.92, 1.00, 1.05, 1.08 (±8 %), giving 20 utterances of 0.30 s per vowel segment with 20 ms silence at each end. A 5 dB SNR and a 0 dB SNR copy of each utterance were formed by adding white Gaussian noise.

**Conditioning and framing.** Pre-emphasis H(z) = 1 − 0.97 z⁻¹ compensates the glottal −6 dB/octave tilt; each signal is blocked into 25 ms (200-sample) frames with a 10 ms (80-sample) hop and a Hamming window, then transformed by a 512-point FFT, giving 15.6 Hz bin spacing and a 100 Hz frame rate.

**Feature extractors, on identical frames.** (a) MFCC: 26 triangular mel-spaced filters over 0–4000 Hz, log compression, orthonormal DCT-II, 13 coefficients. (b) LPCC: a 12th-order LPC vector per frame from the autocorrelation method with the Levinson–Durbin recursion, converted to 13 cepstral coefficients. © DFT log-spectrum: log magnitude of the FFT sampled at 13 mel-spaced bin centres. All streams pass through cepstral mean normalisation (CMN) to remove the fixed channel and gain offset. Formants come from the roots of A(z): each complex root z gives F = (Fs/2π) arg z and B = −(Fs/π) ln|z|; roots with B < 500 Hz and 90 < F < 3800 Hz are kept.

**Classifier and metrics.** An unknown utterance takes the label of the template with the smallest duration-normalised DTW distance. (C1) matched: three templates per word from the other three speakers, all 20 utterances tested. (C2) mismatched: 5 dB SNR signals, one template per word from speaker 1, remaining 15 tested. (C3) the same single-template protocol at 0 dB SNR. The LPC order was swept over p = 8, 10, 12, 14, and the fitted LPC envelope was compared with the known envelope of the resonator cascade.

## 3. Technique Used

**Short-time analysis.** Speech may be treated as stationary over 20–30 ms, which justifies the 25 ms frame. Fs = 8 kHz keeps the first three or four formants – enough to separate vowels – at half the data rate of 16 kHz, and the 10 ms hop resolves the transitions of /ai/ and /ia/. The Hamming window suppresses the sidelobe leakage that would otherwise let strong low-frequency harmonics mask higher formants, at the cost of a wider main lobe, and the zero-padded 512-point FFT supplies the interpolated spectrum.

$$ X[k] = Σ n=0..N−1 x[n] e^(−j2πkn/N) $$

**All-pole modelling.** Each frame is modelled by H(z) = G/A(z), whose coefficients describe the vocal-tract resonances of that instant. They are found by minimising the mean-squared prediction error, i.e. by solving the Yule–Walker normal equations with the Levinson–Durbin recursion in O(p²) operations; the recursion also yields the prediction-error energy used as a voicing indicator and convergence check.

$$A(z) = 1 − Σ k=1..p aₖ z^(−k) ;   Σ k=1..p aₖ r[|i−k|] = −r[i],  i = 1..p$$

**Cepstral analysis.** The real cepstrum c[n] = IDFT{log|X[k]|} turns the convolution of excitation and vocal tract into a sum, so low-quefrency coefficients carry the envelope and the high-quefrency peak carries pitch. LPC parameters are mapped into the same domain by the recursion below, giving a smooth, well-behaved feature.

cₘ = −aₘ − Σ k=1..m−1 (k/m) cₖ a_(m−k) (1 ≤ m ≤ p);   cₘ = −Σ k=1..p (k/m) cₖ a_(m−k) (m > p)

**MFCC and normalisation.** The mel scale, mel(f) = 2595 log₁₀(1 + f/700), compresses the frequency axis to match auditory resolution; the 26 triangular filters average the pitch harmonics instead of tracking them and cut dimensionality from 256 bins to 26 channels; the logarithm equalises the dynamic range; and the DCT-II decorrelates the channels so 13 coefficients carry almost all the envelope information. A linear bank would waste as many channels above 3 kHz as below 1 kHz, where almost no speech energy lies – the comparison with the linear-spaced DFT log-spectrum quantifies that benefit. CMN subtracts the per-utterance mean of each coefficient, cancelling fixed gain and channel response. Delta and delta–delta coefficients were available through the regression formula below but are deliberately excluded, so the comparison isolates the front end.

Δcₜ = Σ n=−N..N n c_(t+n) / (2 Σ n=1..N n²)

**Pattern matching.** The same word is spoken at different rates, so feature sequences are aligned by DTW, which minimises the accumulated local distance over all monotonic paths and normalises by path length, giving a duration-independent score. Vector quantisation with the LBG algorithm and HMM Viterbi decoding (Module II) are not used here because with a few training utterances per word the non-parametric template matcher needs no parameter estimation. Everything was coded in Python 3 with NumPy, as required by CO5.

D(i,j) = d(i,j) + min[D(i−1,j), D(i,j−1), D(i−1,j−1)]

## 5. Advantages of the Solution

**Compact and interpretable.** The 12 LPC coefficients are a physically meaningful description of the vocal tract; the same coefficients give both the 13-dimensional feature vector and the formants, so analysis cost is shared. **Speaker-robust with a tiny training set.** Mel cepstra plus CMN reached 100 % matched and 100 % with one 0 dB template per word – no training, statistical model or parameter estimation. **Duration-independent and cheap.** DTW needs no equal-length segmentation and handles different speaking rates; a 512-point FFT, an order-12 recursion and a short DTW pass are real-time capable on a CPU, and the whole pipeline is about two hundred lines of NumPy (CO5). **Every stage is checkable.** Because the true formants are known, model error (5.3 dB), formant error (18 Hz) and recognition score are measured separately, so a failure can be attributed to the model, the feature or the classifier.

## 6. Disadvantages and Limitations

**Stationarity assumption.** The 25 ms frame hides transients, so bursts and the fast /ai/ transitions are smeared over two or three frames. **Model mismatch.** An all-pole model cannot represent the zeros of nasals and fricatives, and root-based formant estimation is order- and noise-sensitive: spurious poles appear and close formants may merge into one wide pole pair — exactly the cause of the 29.6 % F1 error for /u/, whose F1 and F2 are only 570 Hz apart. **Information discarded.** MFCC and LPCC drop phase and fine spectral detail; CMN discards per-utterance level information that is useful for very short words, and the static-only features used here ignore the delta coefficients that would encode the glide direction of /ai/ explicitly. **Classifier limits.** DTW is quadratic in the number of frames, returns a distance rather than a probability, does not scale to large vocabularies or connected speech, has no language model, and assumes the utterance endpoints are known, whereas real recordings need energy-based endpoint detection; VQ with the LBG algorithm and HMM recognition (Module II) would supply the statistical generalisation the template matcher lacks.

**Evidence limits.** The benchmark is synthetic – 5 words, 20 utterances, four simulated speakers and stationary white noise – so the absolute accuracies are optimistic and are not comparable with results on real corpora; only the ordering of the front ends is meaningful. Reverberation and telephone channels were not tested, and the video topics of lectures 18–21 are not exercised by this speech-only problem.

## Conclusion

A complete pre-mid-semester speech pipeline was implemented and measured. Short-time spectral estimation, all-pole modelling, cepstral representations, cepstral normalisation and dynamic time warping together classify a five-word isolated vocabulary of four speakers, and the mel-cepstral front end was the most robust of the three in the matched and in both mismatched conditions. LPC is the most economical choice when formants are also needed, and the DFT log-spectrum is the weakest because it preserves pitch structure. The next steps – delta features, LBG vector quantisation or HMM models, and evaluation on a real corpus – belong to the post-mid-semester part of the syllabus.
