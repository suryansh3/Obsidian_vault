### TOPIC 1: THE CLASSICAL APPROACH ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1.1 Basic Features of Classical Theory

Classical economics (18th–19th century) rests on these pillars:

**1. Microeconomic Foundations:** Agents (firms, households) are optimizers. Markets are driven by supply and demand.

**2. Laissez-Faire:** Minimal government intervention. Adam Smith's three legitimate government duties: national defense, justice, and public infrastructure.

**3. Invisible Hand (Adam Smith):** Individual self-interest leads to collective welfare automatically.

**4. Continuous Market Clearing:** Prices and wages are fully flexible. Markets always clear — no involuntary unemployment.

**5. Perfect Competition:** Markets operate competitively; output is always at the full employment level.

**6. Say's Law:** "Supply creates its own demand." Every production generates sufficient income to purchase that output. No deficiency of aggregate demand is possible.

**7. Neutrality of Money:** Money is only a medium of exchange. Changes in money supply affect only nominal variables (prices, wages), not real variables (output, employment).

#### 1.2 Determination of Output and Employment

Based on assumptions: agents are optimizers, have perfect knowledge, and operate in competitive markets.

**Labour Market Equilibrium:**
- Labour demand: MPN = W/P (Marginal Product of Labour = Real Wage)
- Labour supply: Ns = g(W/P) — rises with real wage
- Equilibrium: Full employment (NF) at real wage (W/P)*

**Output Determination:**
Y = F(K, N) — Production function with capital (K) and labour (N)

At full employment NF, output is YF (full employment output). The Aggregate Supply (AS) curve is **vertical** at YF.

#### 1.3 Quantity Theory of Money (QTM)

**Fisher's Equation of Exchange:**
MV = PT (transaction version)
MV = PY (income version)

Where: M = Money supply, V = Velocity of circulation, P = Price level, Y = Real output

**Key assumptions:**
- V is constant (determined by payment habits)
- Y is fixed at full employment level

**Implication:** P = (V/Y) × M — Price level is proportional to money supply. A 4x increase in M → 4x increase in P. QTM is thus a **theory of price level**.

#### 1.4 Classical Dichotomy

The separation of real variables (output, employment, real wage) from nominal variables (prices, nominal wages).

- Real variables are determined by supply-side factors (technology, capital, population)
- Nominal variables are determined by money supply
- Changes in money supply do NOT affect real variables

**Neutrality of Money:** Follows from classical dichotomy. Increase in M → proportionate increase in P and W, but real wage (W/P) unchanged → employment and output unchanged.

#### 1.5 Saving-Investment Equilibrium

- Interest rate is determined in the capital (loanable funds) market, not the money market
- Saving and investment depend on the real interest rate
- Increase in money supply shifts both SS and II curves proportionally → real interest rate and real S/I unchanged

### TOPIC 2: THE KEYNESIAN MODEL ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 2.1 Keynesian vs. Classical Theory

| Feature | Classical | Keynesian |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Price flexibility | Fully flexible | Sticky/rigid |
| Employment | Always full | Can be below full |
| AD deficiency | Impossible (Say's Law) | Possible |
| Money | Neutral | Non-neutral in short run |
| Policy | Unnecessary | Essential (govt intervention) |
| Time horizon | Long run | Short run |
| AS curve | Vertical | Upward sloping (SR) |

#### 2.2 Three-Sector Keynesian Model

**Three sectors:** Households (C), Firms (I), Government (G)

**Equilibrium:** Y = C + I + G

**Consumption Function:** C = a + bYd where Yd = Y - T (disposable income)

**With proportional tax** (t = tax rate): Yd = Y(1-t)

**Equilibrium output:**
Y* = (a + I + G - bT) / (1 - b)    [lump-sum tax]
Y* = (a + I + G) / (1 - b(1-t))   [proportional tax]

**Tax Multiplier:** ΔY/ΔT = -b/(1-b)  (negative: taxes reduce income)

**Government Spending Multiplier:** ΔY/ΔG = 1/(1-b)

#### 2.3 Balanced Budget Multiplier

When government increases both G and T by the same amount (ΔG = ΔT):

Net effect on Y = ΔG × [1/(1-b)] + ΔT × [-b/(1-b)]
= ΔG × [1/(1-b)] - ΔG × [b/(1-b)]
= ΔG × [(1-b)/(1-b)]
= **ΔG = 1**

**Balanced Budget Multiplier = 1.** Even a balanced budget expansion increases output by the full amount of spending.

#### 2.4 Proportional Tax Effects

With a proportional tax at rate t:
- Multiplier = 1/(1 - b(1-t)) < 1/(1-b)
- Proportional tax acts as an **automatic stabilizer** — it reduces the multiplier, dampening both booms and recessions.

### TOPIC 3: THE IS-LM MODEL (Neoclassical Synthesis) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 3.1 The IS Curve

**IS curve:** Locus of all (Y, r) combinations where the goods market is in equilibrium (I = S or Y = C + I + G).

**Derivation:**
Investment: I = I₀ - br (inversely related to interest rate)
Goods market equilibrium: Y = C + I + G
Y = a + bY + I₀ - br + G
Y(1-b) = a + I₀ - br + G
**Y = (a + I₀ + G)/(1-b) - br/(1-b)**

This is the IS curve: **downward sloping in (Y, r) space**.

**Factors shifting IS curve (rightward):**
- Increase in autonomous consumption (a)
- Increase in autonomous investment (I₀)
- Increase in government spending (G)
- Decrease in taxes (T)

**Slope of IS:** Steeper when MPC (b) is small or investment is interest-inelastic (b is small).

#### 3.2 The LM Curve

**LM curve:** Locus of all (Y, r) combinations where the money market is in equilibrium (Md = Ms).

**Money demand:** L = kY - hr (transactions demand rises with Y; speculative demand falls with r)

**Equilibrium:** M/P = kY - hr → **r = (kY - M/P)/h**

LM curve is **upward sloping in (Y, r) space**.

**Factors shifting LM curve (rightward):**
- Increase in money supply (M)
- Decrease in price level (P)

#### 3.3 IS-LM Equilibrium

Both curves intersect at equilibrium (Y*, r*). This is simultaneous equilibrium in goods AND money markets.

**Points outside equilibrium:**
- Point above IS, below LM: excess supply in goods market, excess demand in money market
- Point below IS, above LM: excess demand in goods market, excess supply in money market

**Automatic adjustment:** The economy moves back to equilibrium through changes in output (goods market) and interest rate (money market).

**Crowding Out Effect:** Expansionary fiscal policy (G↑) shifts IS right → higher r → reduces I → partially offsets rise in G. Degree depends on interest sensitivity of investment and slope of LM.

### TOPIC 4: OPEN ECONOMY MACROECONOMICS ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 4.1 The Mundell-Fleming Model

Extension of IS-LM to an open economy. Adds the Balance of Payments (BP) curve.

**BP curve:** Locus of (Y, r) combinations where BP is in equilibrium.
BP = Current Account + Capital Account = 0

**Under perfect capital mobility:** BP curve is horizontal at the world interest rate r*.

#### 4.2 Fiscal Policy Under Flexible Exchange Rates

**Under flexible exchange rates with perfect capital mobility:**

1. Govt increases G → IS shifts right → Y↑, r↑
2. Higher r attracts foreign capital → Capital inflow
3. Demand for domestic currency rises → Exchange rate appreciates
4. Appreciation makes exports expensive and imports cheap → NX falls (CA deteriorates)
5. IS shifts back left until original equilibrium is restored

**Conclusion: Fiscal policy is completely ineffective under flexible exchange rates with perfect capital mobility.** (Mundell-Fleming result)

**Policy option available:** Monetary policy. Increase in money supply → LM shifts right → r falls → exchange rate depreciates → NX rises → output increases. **Monetary policy is fully effective under flexible exchange rates.**

#### 4.3 Balance of Payments Adjustment

**Disequilibrium in BoP → Change in foreign exchange reserves:**

- **BoP surplus:** Demand for domestic currency > supply → Central bank buys foreign currency → Forex reserves increase → Money supply increases
- **BoP deficit:** Demand for domestic currency < supply → Central bank sells foreign currency → Forex reserves decrease → Money supply decreases

Under fixed exchange rates, forex reserves act as the automatic adjustment mechanism.

### TOPIC 5: INFLATION AND UNEMPLOYMENT ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 5.1 Phillips Curve

**Original Phillips Curve (A.W. Phillips, 1958):**
Inverse relationship between inflation (π) and unemployment (u):
π = -α(u - u*)

**Adaptive Expectations and the Phillips Curve:**

Under adaptive expectations, workers adjust their inflation expectations based on past inflation:
πe(t) = πe(t-1) + λ(π(t-1) - πe(t-1))

**Expectations-Augmented Phillips Curve (Friedman-Phelps):**
π = πe - α(u - u*) + ε

Where πe = expected inflation, u* = natural rate of unemployment

**Key Insight:** There is NO long-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment. In the long run, the economy is at the natural rate of unemployment (NAIRU) regardless of inflation. The long-run Phillips curve is **vertical**.

**Short-run vs. Long-run:** In the short run, unexpected inflation can temporarily reduce unemployment below u*. But workers eventually revise their expectations upward → wages rise → unemployment returns to u*. This leads to the **accelerationist hypothesis**.

#### 5.2 Natural Rate of Unemployment

The unemployment rate consistent with stable inflation. Determined by structural factors:
- Frictional unemployment (job search)
- Structural unemployment (skill mismatch)
- Institutional factors (minimum wage, unions)

#### 5.3 Stagflation

Simultaneous high inflation AND high unemployment. Classical tools (demand management) cannot solve both simultaneously. Associated with supply shocks (e.g., 1970s oil crisis).

### TOPIC 6: RATIONAL EXPECTATIONS & LUCAS SUPPLY FUNCTION ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 6.1 Rational Expectations Hypothesis (REH)

Economic agents form expectations using ALL available information, including the structure of the economic model itself.

E[π(t+1) | Ωt] = π*(t+1)

Where Ωt = all information available at time t.

**Key features:**
- Expectations are model-consistent
- Agents do not make systematic forecasting errors
- Surprises are random (white noise)
- Agents learn from past mistakes and correct them

**Policy implication (Lucas Critique):** Policy rules based on past behavioral relationships break down when agents anticipate the policy, because they will change their behavior. Historical data cannot be used to evaluate new policies.

#### 6.2 Lucas Supply Function

**Robert Lucas:** Output deviates from its natural level ONLY due to unexpected changes in price level (surprises).

**Lucas Supply Function:**
Y = Y* + b(P - Pe)

Where: Y* = natural output, P = actual price level, Pe = expected price level

**Key ideas:**
- In the long run, P = Pe → Y = Y* (economy returns to natural output)
- Short-run output fluctuations are caused by agents misinterpreting price signals
- **Signal extraction problem:** Firms cannot distinguish between relative price changes and aggregate price changes (the "island model")
- If money supply increases and is anticipated, Pe adjusts → no real effect. Only **unanticipated** monetary policy has real effects.

**Why upward sloping in the short run:** When P > Pe, producers think the relative price of their good has risen, so they increase supply. This is a misperception — aggregate price level has risen, not their relative price. Hence short-run AS is upward sloping.

#### 6.3 Policy Ineffectiveness Theorem (Sargent-Wallace)

If expectations are rational and markets clear, **systematic (anticipated) monetary policy has NO real effects** on output and employment.

Only **unsystematic (random) policy** can affect real variables, but such policy is undesirable. This effectively undermines the case for active stabilization policy.

### TOPIC 7: RAMSEY-CASS-KOOPMANS (RCK) MODEL ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 7.1 Overview

The RCK model is the foundational model of optimal growth/dynamic optimization. It replaces the ad hoc saving assumption in the Solow model with optimizing household behavior.

#### 7.2 Central Planner's Problem

The social planner maximizes the lifetime utility of a representative household:

**Maximize:** ∫₀^∞ u(c(t)) · e^(-ρt) · L(t)/H dt

Subject to: k̇ = f(k) - c - (n+δ)k

Where:
- c = per capita consumption
- k = per capita capital
- ρ = rate of time preference (discount rate)
- n = population growth rate
- δ = depreciation rate
- f(k) = per capita production function
- L(t)/H = ratio of population to households

**Utility function:** u(c) = c^(1-θ)/(1-θ), where θ = coefficient of relative risk aversion / inverse of elasticity of intertemporal substitution.

**Key equations:**

**Euler Equation (Keynes-Ramsey Rule):**
ċ/c = [f'(k) - ρ - δ] / θ = [r - ρ] / θ

This says consumption grows when the return to capital (r = f'(k) - δ) exceeds the rate of time preference (ρ).

**Capital accumulation:**
k̇ = f(k) - c - (n+δ)k

#### 7.3 Modified Golden Rule

At the **steady state:** ċ = 0 and k̇ = 0

From Euler equation: f'(k*) - δ = ρ → f'(k*) = ρ + δ

**Modified Golden Rule:** k* satisfies f'(k*) = ρ + δ + nθ (with population growth)

This contrasts with the **Golden Rule (Solow):** f'(k) = δ + n (maximizes consumption, ignores time preference).

**The modified golden rule capital stock is LOWER than the golden rule capital stock** because impatient households discount the future (ρ > 0).

**Phase Diagram:**
- ċ = 0 locus: vertical line at k* (where f'(k) = ρ+δ)
- k̇ = 0 locus: c = f(k) - (n+δ)k (the consumption level that keeps k unchanged)
- Saddle path: unique path converging to steady state (E)
- Unstable: paths diverge to zero consumption or zero capital

#### 7.4 Decentralized Household Problem

Households maximize utility taking prices (r, w) as given:

**Household's budget constraint:**
ȧ = (r - n)a + w - c

Where a = assets per person, w = wage

**Euler equation is the same:** ċ/c = (r - ρ)/θ

**Transversality condition:** lim(t→∞) a(t)e^(-∫₀ᵗ r(s)ds) = 0 (no Ponzi scheme)

In the decentralized equilibrium, r = f'(k) - δ and w = f(k) - kf'(k), and the equilibrium replicates the central planner's solution.

### TOPIC 8: BUSINESS CYCLES ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 8.1 Features of Business Cycles

A business cycle refers to the recurrent but irregular fluctuations in economic activity around its long-run trend.

**Phases:**
1. **Expansion/Boom:** Rising output, employment, income, investment
2. **Peak:** Maximum level of activity
3. **Contraction/Recession:** Falling output, rising unemployment
4. **Trough:** Minimum level of activity

**Characteristics:**
- Recurrent but not periodic (irregular intervals)
- Co-movement of macroeconomic variables (output, employment, investment, consumption all move together)
- Investment is more volatile than consumption
- Procyclical: prices, profits, investment, employment
- Countercyclical: unemployment

**Dating of Business Cycles:**
NBER (National Bureau of Economic Research) method: A recession = two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth. NBER dates peaks and troughs based on a broader set of indicators.

#### 8.2 Indicators of Business Cycles

**Leading indicators** (signal future activity):
- Stock prices
- New orders for capital goods
- Building permits
- Money supply (M2)
- Consumer confidence index

**Coincident indicators** (move with the cycle):
- GDP/industrial production
- Employment levels
- Personal income
- Manufacturing sales

**Lagging indicators** (confirm after the fact):
- Unemployment rate
- Bank loans outstanding
- CPI for services
- Average prime lending rate

#### 8.3 Samuelson's Multiplier-Accelerator Model

Interaction of the multiplier and accelerator generates cyclical fluctuations.

**Multiplier:** ΔY = (1/(1-b)) × ΔG

**Accelerator:** Investment depends on changes in output: I(t) = v[Y(t-1) - Y(t-2)]

**Samuelson's difference equation:**
Y(t) = (b+v)Y(t-1) - vY(t-2) + A

The behavior of this system depends on (b+v):
- If (b+v) > 1 and v < 1: Damped oscillations → cycles that converge
- If (b+v) = 1: Regular undamped cycles
- If (b+v) > 1 and v > 1: Explosive oscillations
- If (b+v) < 1: Monotonic convergence (no cycles)

The interaction of multiplier and accelerator naturally generates cyclical behavior.

#### 8.4 Real Business Cycle (RBC) Theory

**Key claims:**
- Business cycles are caused by **real shocks**, primarily technology/productivity shocks (Total Factor Productivity — TFP)
- Fluctuations are **optimal responses** of rational agents to these shocks
- There is no role for money or demand shocks
- Markets always clear — no involuntary unemployment

**A Simple RBC Model:**

Households maximize: Σ βᵗ [u(Cₜ) + v(Lₜ)] (utility of consumption + leisure)

Firms: Yₜ = Aₜ F(Kₜ, Nₜ) where Aₜ is stochastic productivity

Technology shock: ln Aₜ = ρ ln Aₜ₋₁ + εₜ (AR(1) process)

**Intertemporal substitution of labour:** When productivity is high (wages temporarily high), agents work more (substitute leisure today for leisure tomorrow). This generates procyclical employment.

**Policy implication:** Since cycles are optimal, government stabilization policy is **welfare-reducing**. The right policy response to a recession is to do nothing.

**Criticisms of RBC:**
- Technology shocks that are large enough to explain observed fluctuations are unrealistic
- Ignores monetary factors (recessions following monetary contractions are hard to explain)
- Intertemporal substitution of labour is empirically weak

### TOPIC 9: NOMINAL AND REAL RIGIDITIES ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 9.1 Why Rigidities Matter

In classical models, prices and wages are fully flexible, and markets always clear. In reality, prices and wages adjust slowly. These rigidities explain why output can deviate from its natural level and why aggregate demand matters.

#### 9.2 Nominal Rigidities

Prices and wages do not adjust instantaneously to changes in nominal variables (money supply).

**Menu Costs:** Fixed costs of changing prices (printing new menus, updating catalogs). Even small menu costs can prevent firms from adjusting prices, especially when the optimal price change is small. In the aggregate, even small individual rigidities can cause large macroeconomic effects.

**Staggered price/wage setting:** Contracts are set at different times. Not all prices adjust simultaneously. This creates "stickiness" in the aggregate price level.

**Efficiency wages (nominal wage rigidity):** Firms pay wages above market-clearing levels to:
- Increase worker productivity (higher morale, effort)
- Reduce worker turnover
- Attract higher quality workers (adverse selection)

Wage rigidity above the market-clearing level results in involuntary unemployment.

#### 9.3 Real Rigidities

Real rigidities refer to factors that make the **real wage or real price** unresponsive to changes in demand/supply conditions.

Real rigidities amplify the effects of nominal rigidities: if there are real rigidities, firms have less incentive to change their prices even when there are no menu costs.

**Types of Real Rigidities:**

**In the Labour Market:**
- **Efficiency wages:** Real wage is set above market-clearing level to elicit effort. Wage cuts reduce worker productivity — it is not in the firm's interest to cut real wages during recessions.
- **Insider-outsider model:** "Insiders" (employed workers) have bargaining power and set wages to benefit themselves, ignoring the interests of "outsiders" (unemployed). Wages are sticky downward.
- **Long-term contracts / implicit contracts:** Workers value wage stability; contracts lock in nominal wages → real wage adjusts slowly.

**In the Goods Market:**
- **Kinked demand curve:** Under oligopoly, firms fear that competitors will not follow price increases but will follow price cuts. This makes demand kinky at the current price → firms are reluctant to change prices.
- **Customer market model:** Firms fear losing customers by raising prices, creating stickiness.
- **Cost-based pricing / markup pricing:** Firms set prices as markup over costs. Prices change only when costs change significantly.

**In the Credit Market:**
- **Credit rationing:** At high interest rates, adverse selection worsens (only risky borrowers apply). Banks prefer to ration credit rather than raise rates. This means interest rate may not clear the credit market.
- **Collateral constraints:** Borrowing is limited by asset values. When asset prices fall, borrowing capacity falls, reducing investment.

### TOPIC 10: SEARCH THEORY AND UNEMPLOYMENT ⭐⭐⭐

#### 10.1 Search Theory

Traditional models treat unemployment as either frictional or structural. Search theory provides a micro-foundation for frictional unemployment.

**Key idea:** Labour markets are characterized by frictions — it takes time for workers and firms to find each other and agree on a wage. Unemployment is the outcome of this search process.

**Worker's decision:** Accept a job offer if the offered wage ≥ reservation wage (w̄).

**Reservation wage** is determined by: Benefits while searching + Expected gains from continuing to search

**Optimal stopping rule:** Keep searching as long as the expected gain from search > current offer.

**Implication:** Higher unemployment benefits → higher reservation wage → longer unemployment duration → higher unemployment rate.

#### 10.2 Search and Matching Model (Diamond-Mortensen-Pissarides)

**Matching function:** M = m(U, V) — Matches depend on unemployment (U) and vacancies (V).

**Job finding rate:** q = M/U = m(1, V/U) = m(θ) where θ = V/U is "market tightness"

**Vacancy filling rate:** q' = M/V

**Steady-state unemployment:** At steady state, flows into unemployment = flows out:
s(1-u) = q(θ)u → u = s/(s + q(θ))

Where s = separation rate.

**Wage determination:** Nash bargaining between worker and firm gives wage that splits surplus.

### TOPIC 11: MONETARY POLICY ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 11.1 Objectives of Monetary Policy

1. Price stability (primary objective of modern central banks)
2. Full employment / output stability
3. Exchange rate stability
4. Financial system stability

#### 11.2 Instruments of Monetary Policy

**Quantitative Instruments (General):**

**Bank Rate / Repo Rate:** Interest rate at which RBI lends to commercial banks. Increase → borrowing costs rise → money supply contracts → inflation falls. Decrease → opposite.

**Open Market Operations (OMO):** RBI buys/sells government securities.
- Buying securities → injects reserves → money supply increases
- Selling securities → absorbs reserves → money supply decreases

**Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR):** Fraction of deposits banks must hold as cash with RBI.
- Increase CRR → banks can lend less → money supply contracts
- Decrease CRR → banks can lend more → money supply expands

**Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR):** Fraction of deposits banks must hold in specified liquid assets.

**Qualitative Instruments (Selective):**
- Margin requirements
- Moral suasion
- Credit rationing
- Selective credit controls
- Direct lending guidelines

#### 11.3 Measures of Money Supply (Money Stock)

| Aggregate | Components |
|-----------|------------|
| **M0** (Reserve Money) | Currency in circulation + Banker's deposits with RBI + Other deposits with RBI |
| **M1** (Narrow Money) | Currency with public + Demand deposits + Other deposits with RBI |
| **M2** | M1 + Savings deposits with post offices |
| **M3** (Broad Money) | M1 + Time deposits with banks |
| **M4** | M3 + All deposits with post offices |

**Money Multiplier:**
m = M/H = (1 + c) / (c + r)

Where c = currency-deposit ratio, r = reserve-deposit ratio, H = high-powered money

#### 11.4 Transmission Mechanism of Monetary Policy

How does monetary policy affect the real economy? Main channels:

**1. Interest Rate Channel (Traditional):** M↑ → r↓ → I↑ → Y↑

**2. Credit Channel:**
- **Bank lending channel:** M↑ → bank reserves↑ → bank loans↑ → I↑ → Y↑
- **Balance sheet channel:** M↑ → asset prices↑ → collateral value↑ → borrowing↑ → I↑ → Y↑

**3. Exchange Rate Channel:** M↑ → r↓ → capital outflow → exchange rate depreciates → NX↑ → Y↑

**4. Asset Price Channel (Tobin's q):** M↑ → stock prices↑ → q = (market value of capital)/(replacement cost) → if q > 1, firms issue new equity cheaply → I↑

**5. Wealth Effect:** M↑ → asset prices↑ → household wealth↑ → C↑ → Y↑

### TOPIC 12: FISCAL POLICY ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 12.1 Discretionary Fiscal Policy

**Discretionary fiscal policy:** Deliberate changes in government spending (G) or taxes (T) to stabilize the economy.

**Expansionary fiscal policy (to cure recession):**
- Increase G: Shifts IS rightward → Y↑, r↑
- Decrease T: Increases disposable income → C↑ → shifts IS rightward → Y↑
- Using the multiplier: ΔY = (1/(1-b)) × ΔG or ΔY = (-b/(1-b)) × ΔT

**Contractionary fiscal policy (to cure inflation):**
- Decrease G
- Increase T

**Inflationary gap:** Y* > Yf (actual output > full employment output)
→ Reduce G or increase T to close the gap.

**Recessionary gap:** Y* < Yf (actual output < full employment output)
→ Increase G or reduce T.

#### 12.2 Time Lags in Discretionary Fiscal Policy

**1. Recognition lag:** Time taken to recognize that a recession/boom has begun.

**2. Decision lag (Inside lag):** Time for government to legislate and approve fiscal measures. This is the most significant lag for fiscal policy. Legislative process is slow.

**3. Implementation lag:** Time between policy decision and actual expenditure.

**4. Impact lag (Outside lag):** Time for policy to affect the economy through multiplier effects.

These lags mean that by the time fiscal policy takes effect, economic conditions may have changed → stabilization policy may itself be destabilizing.

#### 12.3 Non-Discretionary Fiscal Policy (Automatic Stabilizers)

Mechanisms that automatically cushion the economy without deliberate policy action:

- **Progressive income tax:** When income falls, tax revenue falls more (lower marginal rate) → disposable income cushioned.
- **Unemployment insurance:** Transfer payments automatically increase during recessions.
- **Corporate profit taxes:** Fall sharply in recessions.

Automatic stabilizers reduce the multiplier effect, thus dampening both booms and recessions.

**Advantage over discretionary policy:** No decision or implementation lag.

#### 12.4 Budget Deficit and Debt-GDP Ratio

**Budget deficit:** G > T → government must borrow.

**Debt-GDP ratio (D/Y):** Changes according to:
Δ(D/Y) = (r - g)(D/Y) + (G - T)/Y

Where r = real interest rate, g = economic growth rate.

**Danger of high Debt-GDP ratio:**
1. **Debt servicing burden:** High interest payments crowd out productive expenditure.
2. **Crowding out:** Government borrowing raises interest rates → private investment falls.
3. **Fiscal unsustainability:** If r > g, debt-GDP ratio keeps rising → debt becomes unsustainable.
4. **Ricardian Equivalence (counter-argument):** Rational agents save to pay future taxes → deficits don't stimulate demand. But this holds only under strict assumptions.

**Factors increasing Debt-GDP ratio:**
- High real interest rate (r↑)
- Low economic growth (g↓)
- Primary budget deficit (G > T)
- Exchange rate depreciation (for external debt)

#### 12.5 Dynamic Inconsistency and Loss Function

**Dynamic inconsistency (Kydland-Prescott, 1977):** A policy that is optimal at time t (when announced) may not be optimal to actually implement at time t+1.

**Classic example:** Inflation and unemployment.
- At t=0, central bank announces low inflation target. Workers sign contracts based on this.
- At t=1, given low expected inflation (πe low), there is a temptation to inflate (push π above πe) to reduce unemployment below the natural rate.
- But rational agents anticipate this → they set high πe from the start → inflation with no benefit to employment.

**Loss function of central bank:**
L = (π - π*)² + λ(u - u*)²

Where π* = target inflation, u* = target unemployment, λ = weight on unemployment.

The time-consistent policy leads to an "inflation bias" — inflation higher than the socially optimal level.

**Solution:** Credible commitment, independent central bank, inflation targeting.

### TOPIC 13: FISCAL SUSTAINABILITY (Debt-GDP) ⭐⭐⭐

#### 13.1 Ricardian Equivalence

**Ricardian Equivalence (Robert Barro):** Under certain conditions, it does not matter whether the government finances its expenditure through taxes or bonds.

**Logic:** If government cuts taxes and borrows today, it must raise taxes in the future. Rational households anticipate this future tax increase, save the tax cut, and increase their private saving by exactly the amount of government borrowing. Hence, national saving is unchanged and interest rates / investment are unaffected.

**Conditions for Ricardian Equivalence to hold:**
- Perfect capital markets
- No liquidity constraints
- Rational, infinitely-lived agents (or altruistic parents who care about children)
- Lump-sum taxes

**Why it may not hold:**
- Liquidity-constrained households spend the tax cut
- Finite-lived agents
- Uncertainty about future taxes
- Distortionary taxes

### Q1. Explain the Classical Theory of Macroeconomics with its salient features. Examine the relevance of these features in the present context. (Dec 2023 — 20 marks)

**Answer:**

**Introduction:** Classical economics, rooted in the ideas of Adam Smith, Ricardo, Say, and Mill, represents the dominant paradigm from the 18th to early 20th century. It provides a supply-side explanation of output, employment, and prices.

**Salient Features:**

1. **Flexible Prices and Wages:** The cornerstone assumption. Market-clearing is continuous, ensuring full employment at all times.

2. **Say's Law:** Supply creates its own demand. Every act of production generates income equal to the value of output, which is then spent. There can be no general glut or demand deficiency.

3. **Quantity Theory of Money:** MV = PY. Given full employment output (Y fixed) and stable velocity (V fixed), price level (P) is proportional to money supply (M). Money is therefore a "veil" over the real economy.

4. **Classical Dichotomy:** Real variables (output, employment, relative prices) are determined by real forces (technology, capital, labor). Nominal variables are determined by money supply. Changes in M affect only P, not Y.

5. **Neutrality of Money:** Following from the dichotomy, money supply changes do not affect real variables. A doubling of M leads to a doubling of P and W, but W/P (real wage), N, and Y remain unchanged.

6. **Laissez-faire:** Markets function best without government intervention. The "invisible hand" ensures efficient outcomes.

7. **Automatic Market Adjustment:** Full flexibility of prices and wages means any temporary disequilibrium is automatically corrected. There is no role for stabilization policy.

**Relevance in Present Context:**

*Still relevant:*
- QTM provides useful insights on inflation causation — high and persistent inflation is almost always a monetary phenomenon (as in hyperinflations).
- Classical dichotomy is a useful long-run benchmark.
- Laissez-faire ideas underpin modern market economies and free trade.

*Limitations in present context:*
- **Prices and wages are sticky** in the short run. Empirical evidence strongly shows wages are downwardly rigid (efficiency wages, contracts). This leads to persistent unemployment.
- **The 2008 financial crisis** showed demand deficiency can be severe, contrary to Say's Law.
- **The 1930s Great Depression** was the original refutation of classical theory — massive involuntary unemployment persisted.
- **Monetary policy does matter** — evidence shows unanticipated monetary expansions have real effects, especially in the short run.

**Conclusion:** Classical theory remains a powerful long-run benchmark. However, its strict assumptions of perfect flexibility and continuous market clearing make it inappropriate for short-run analysis, where Keynesian and New Keynesian frameworks are more relevant.

### Q2. Discuss the IS-LM Model. Explain how points outside the curves signify disequilibrium and whether the economy reaches equilibrium automatically. (Jun 2024 — 20 marks)

**Answer:**

**IS Curve:** Derived from goods market equilibrium (I = S). Every point on the IS curve represents (Y, r) combinations where the goods market clears.

**Derivation of IS:**
Y = C + I + G
C = a + b(Y-T);  I = I₀ - br
Substituting: Y = a + b(Y-T) + I₀ - br + G
Y(1-b) = a - bT + I₀ + G - br
Y = [a - bT + I₀ + G]/(1-b) - [b/(1-b)]r → **downward sloping.**

**LM Curve:** Derived from money market equilibrium (Md = Ms).
Md = kY - hr;  Ms = M/P
kY - hr = M/P → r = (kY - M/P)/h → **upward sloping.**

**IS-LM Equilibrium:** Both markets are in simultaneous equilibrium at (Y*, r*).

**Points outside the curves:**

**Point above IS (excess supply in goods market):** At given r, Y is too high relative to IS equilibrium. I < S. Firms reduce output → Y falls back toward IS.

**Point below IS (excess demand in goods market):** I > S. Firms increase output → Y rises toward IS.

**Point above LM (excess supply of money):** At given Y, r is too high relative to LM equilibrium. People hold excess money → buy bonds → bond prices rise → r falls toward LM.

**Point below LM (excess demand for money):** People sell bonds → bond prices fall → r rises toward LM.

**Automatic Adjustment:**

Starting from any point, the economy adjusts simultaneously through:
1. Output adjusting to clear the goods market (movement along the Y-axis).
2. Interest rate adjusting to clear the money market (movement along the r-axis).

The adjustment process converges to the unique equilibrium (Y*, r*) where both IS and LM are satisfied. Thus, YES — the economy does reach equilibrium automatically in the IS-LM framework, though it may take time.

**Limitations:** The IS-LM model assumes a closed economy and does not explicitly model the price level or supply side.

### Q3. What are the features of Real Business Cycle (RBC) theory? Describe a simple RBC model. (Dec 2024 — 20 marks)

**Answer:**

**Features of RBC Theory:**

1. **Technology Shocks as Driver:** Business cycles are caused by random shocks to Total Factor Productivity (TFP). These are real shocks (not monetary).

2. **Optimal Response:** Fluctuations represent the optimal response of rational, forward-looking agents to these shocks. There is no market failure.

3. **Market Clearing:** All markets clear at all times. Unemployment is voluntary — agents choose leisure when wages are temporarily low.

4. **Intertemporal Substitution:** Agents shift labour and consumption across time. When productivity (and hence wages) is temporarily high, they work more and save more.

5. **Money is Neutral:** Monetary variables play no role. The model is purely real.

6. **No Stabilization Policy:** Since cycles are efficient, government intervention is counterproductive.

**A Simple RBC Model:**

**Households:** Maximize lifetime utility:
Σ βᵗ [ln(Cₜ) + α ln(1 - Nₜ)]

Subject to: Cₜ + Kₜ₊₁ = Wₜ Nₜ + rₜ Kₜ + (1-δ)Kₜ

**Firms:** Maximize profit given the production technology:
Yₜ = Aₜ Kₜᵅ Nₜ^(1-α)

Profit maximization: rₜ = α(Yₜ/Kₜ) and Wₜ = (1-α)(Yₜ/Nₜ)

**Technology shock:** ln Aₜ = ρ ln Aₜ₋₁ + εₜ, εₜ ~ N(0, σ²)

**When a positive technology shock hits (A↑):**
- Wages and returns to capital temporarily rise
- Households substitute: work more today (intertemporal labour substitution), save more
- Output, employment, investment all rise together → mimics a boom
- Positive co-movement of macroeconomic variables

**When shock reverses:** Wages fall, households work less, output and employment fall → recession.

**Quantitative Evaluation:** Calibration to match key moments of business cycle data (variance of output, consumption, investment; correlations; labour productivity correlations). RBC models generally match many stylized facts but struggle with the behaviour of real wages (they should be strongly procyclical but data shows weaker correlation).

### Q4. Distinguish between Nominal and Real Rigidities. Describe types of real rigidities in an economy. (Dec 2023, Dec 2025 — 20 marks)

**Answer:**

**Distinction:**

| Feature | Nominal Rigidity | Real Rigidity |
|---------|-----------------|---------------|
| Definition | Prices/wages do not adjust to changes in money supply (nominal variables) | Real wages/relative prices unresponsive to changes in demand/supply |
| Example | Menu costs, wage contracts | Efficiency wages, insider-outsider model |
| Effect | Prevents nominal adjustment → real effects of nominal shocks | Reduces incentive to adjust prices even without nominal rigidity |
| Interaction | With real rigidities, even small nominal rigidities cause large output effects | Amplifies nominal rigidities |

**Types of Real Rigidities:**

**In the Labour Market:**

1. **Efficiency Wage Theory:** Firms pay wages above the market-clearing level because higher wages increase worker productivity (reduce shirking, lower turnover, attract better workers). Wage cuts reduce productivity → not in the firm's interest to cut wages even in recessions. Real wages are rigid downward.

2. **Insider-Outsider Model:** "Insiders" (currently employed workers) set wages to benefit themselves. Unions or workers with specific human capital extract rents. Since outsiders (unemployed) have no bargaining power, wages remain above market-clearing, keeping insiders employed and outsiders permanently unemployed.

3. **Implicit Contracts:** Workers are risk-averse and value wage stability. Firms, being less risk-averse (diversified), offer stable wages. This creates real wage rigidity.

**In the Goods Market:**

1. **Kinked Demand Curve (Sweezy):** Under oligopoly, a firm's demand curve has a "kink" at the current price — rivals match price cuts but not price rises. This creates a zone of inelastic marginal revenue, meaning prices are sticky over a range of cost changes.

2. **Customer Market Models:** Firms fear losing customers by raising prices, especially in markets where customers search across firms. Prices become sticky.

3. **Cost-based / Markup Pricing:** Prices are set as a fixed markup over costs. They change only when costs change substantially.

**In the Credit Market:**

1. **Credit Rationing (Stiglitz-Weiss):** At high interest rates, adverse selection intensifies (risky borrowers crowd out safe ones). Banks prefer to ration credit rather than raise interest rates further. This breaks the price (interest rate) clearing mechanism in credit markets.

2. **Collateral Constraints:** Borrowing is limited by asset values. When asset prices fall (e.g., in a recession), collateral value falls, reducing borrowing capacity, further depressing investment — a financial accelerator.

### Q5. Using a Loss Function, explain the concept of Dynamic Inconsistency. (Short answer — 12 marks)

**Answer:**

**Dynamic Inconsistency** (Kydland and Prescott, 1977) arises when an optimal policy announced at one point in time is no longer optimal to implement later, even if no new information has arrived.

**Loss Function of the Central Bank:**
L = (π - π*)² + λ(u - u*)²

Where: π = actual inflation, π* = target inflation (say 0), u = unemployment rate, u* = natural rate of unemployment, λ = relative weight on unemployment stabilization.

**The problem:**

Step 1 (Announcement): The central bank announces π = 0. Workers and firms form contracts based on expected inflation πe = 0.

Step 2 (Temptation): Given that πe = 0 (from contracts), the short-run Phillips curve is:
u = u* - α(π - πe) = u* - α(π - 0)

The central bank now minimizes L with respect to π:
∂L/∂π = 2(π - π*) - 2λα(u - u*) = 0
2π + 2λα²π = 0 + 2λαu*
→ π_optimal = λα/(1+λα²) × u* > 0

So the central bank has an incentive to create inflation above the announced level, because it can temporarily reduce unemployment below u*.

Step 3 (Rational Expectations): Rational agents anticipate this temptation, so they set πe = π_optimal > 0 from the start. Wages are set higher, and unemployment ends up at u* anyway — but with positive inflation.

**Result:** The time-consistent equilibrium features positive inflation but no unemployment gain. This is the **inflation bias**.

**Solutions:**
- Appoint a conservative (inflation-averse) central banker — Rogoff (1985)
- Delegate to an independent central bank with a strict inflation target
- Rules-based policy (e.g., inflation targeting framework)
- Reputation-building through repeated games

### Q6. Discuss the Expectations-Augmented Phillips Curve. (Short answer — 12 marks)

**Answer:**

The **original Phillips curve** showed a stable inverse relationship between wage inflation and unemployment. Samuelson and Solow extended it to the inflation-unemployment trade-off.

However, during the 1970s stagflation, the trade-off broke down — both inflation and unemployment rose simultaneously. This led Friedman (1968) and Phelps (1968) to propose the **expectations-augmented Phillips curve:**

**Equation:**
π = πe - α(u - u*) + ε

Where:
- π = actual inflation
- πe = expected inflation
- u = actual unemployment
- u* = natural rate of unemployment
- α > 0 = slope
- ε = supply shock

**Under adaptive expectations:** πe = π(t-1) (backward-looking)

**Implications:**

1. **Short run:** Given πe fixed, a reduction in unemployment below u* requires higher inflation. The short-run Phillips curve is downward sloping, shifting upward as inflationary expectations rise.

2. **Long run:** In the long run, π = πe (expectations are realized). Substituting: 0 = -α(u - u*) → u = u*. The **long-run Phillips curve is vertical at u* = NAIRU.**

3. **No permanent trade-off:** Any attempt to keep u < u* requires ever-increasing inflation (Friedman's "accelerationist hypothesis").

**Diagram:** Short-run PC (SRPC₁ at πe₁) → if u lowered below u*, π rises → SRPC shifts up to SRPC₂ (at πe₂) → in long run, we move up along the vertical LRPC.

**Policy implication:** There is no long-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment. Attempts to exploit the short-run trade-off lead to the "expectations trap" — ever-rising inflation.

### Q7. Explain the Efficiency of Fiscal Policy in an Open Economy with Flexible Exchange Rate. What policy is available to the government? (Jun 2024 — 20 marks)

**Answer:**

In the Mundell-Fleming model under **perfect capital mobility and flexible exchange rates:**

**Step-by-step analysis of expansionary fiscal policy (G↑):**

1. G↑ → IS curve shifts rightward → aggregate demand increases → output and interest rate tend to rise.

2. Higher interest rate (r > r*) attracts foreign capital → large capital inflow.

3. Capital inflow → increased demand for domestic currency → exchange rate appreciates (domestic currency becomes stronger).

4. Exchange rate appreciation → exports become more expensive → NX falls (trade deficit worsens).

5. Fall in NX shifts IS curve back leftward.

6. Under perfect capital mobility, this process continues until r returns to r* (the world interest rate).

7. Final outcome: IS curve returns to its original position → **output is unchanged. Fiscal policy is completely ineffective.**

**Graphical explanation:**
- Initial IS₀ shifts right to IS₁ due to ΔG.
- Exchange rate appreciation shifts IS₁ back to IS₀ (complete crowding out through trade).
- Net result: Same Y*, same r = r*, but higher G and lower NX (government spending has simply replaced net exports).

**Policy option available — Monetary Policy:**

Under flexible exchange rates, monetary policy is **highly effective:**

1. M↑ → LM shifts right → r tends to fall below r*.
2. Capital outflow → domestic currency depreciates.
3. Depreciation → NX rises → IS shifts right.
4. Final result: Higher output at original r = r*.

**Conclusion:** Under flexible exchange rates and perfect capital mobility, the Mundell-Fleming model delivers the striking result that fiscal policy is ineffective (fully crowded out via the exchange rate) while monetary policy is highly effective.
