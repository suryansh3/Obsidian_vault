# PART 2: DETAILED NOTES (ALL UNITS)

## Unit 1: Classical Theory of Macroeconomics

### Salient Features

1. **Flexible Prices and Wages** – Markets clear continuously; any excess demand/supply eliminated by price adjustments.
2. **Say’s Law of Markets** – “Supply creates its own demand.” Production generates income equal to value of output; no general overproduction.
3. **Quantity Theory of Money** – MV = PY. With V and Y constant, ΔM leads to proportional ΔP. Money is neutral.
4. **Classical Dichotomy** – Real variables (output, employment) determined by real factors; monetary variables (prices) determined separately.
5. **Laissez-Faire** – Minimal government intervention; economy self-regulating via “invisible hand.”
6. **Full Employment** – No involuntary unemployment; any unemployment is voluntary (frictional or structural).

### Determination of Output and Employment

- Labour demand: MPN = W/P (downward sloping)
- Labour supply: Ns = g(W/P) (upward sloping)
- Equilibrium gives full employment Nf and real wage (W/P)*
- Production function Y = F(K, N) yields full employment output Yf
- AS curve vertical at Yf

### Quantity Theory of Money (Fisher’s Equation)

```
MV = PT  or  MV = PY
```
- V = velocity of circulation (constant in short run)
- T = number of transactions (proxied by Y)
- Implication: P = (V/Y) × M → ΔM causes proportional ΔP

### Say’s Law

- In a barter economy, production is for consumption; income spent entirely.
- In money economy, saving is matched by investment (via interest rate flexibility).
- No deficiency of aggregate demand.

### Relevance Today

- **Valid:** Long-run inflation is monetary phenomenon; supply-side matters for growth.
- **Invalid:** Great Depression, 2008 crisis, COVID recession show markets fail to self-correct; unemployment persists; central banks actively manage economies.

## Unit 2: Keynesian Theory of Macroeconomics

### Assumptions

1. Unemployed resources (excess capacity)
2. Rigid wages and prices (sticky downwards)
3. Output is homogeneous; demand-determined
4. Closed economy (initially), later open

### Components of Aggregate Demand

**Consumption Function:**
```
C = a + bY           (two-sector)
C = a + b(Y – T)     (three-sector)
```
- a = autonomous consumption
- b = marginal propensity to consume (0 < b < 1)

**Investment Function:**
```
I = Ī                (autonomous)
I = c + dY – e i     (with interest rate)
```

**Government:** G exogenous, T lump sum or proportional (tY)

### Determination of Equilibrium Output (Three-Sector)

```
Y = C + I + G
Y = a + b(Y – T) + Ī + G
Y – bY = a – bT + Ī + G
Y = (a – bT + Ī + G) / (1 – b)
```

### Multipliers

| Type | Formula |
|------|---------|
| Autonomous expenditure multiplier | 1/(1 – b) |
| Tax multiplier (lump sum) | –b/(1 – b) |
| Balanced budget multiplier | 1 |
| Open economy multiplier | 1/(1 – b + m) |

### Paradox of Thrift

- Desire to save more leads to lower consumption → lower AD → lower income → saving may remain same or fall.
- In short run, thrift is a vice; in long run, it enables growth.

### Liquidity Preference Theory

Demand for money: L = kY – h i (k > 0, h > 0)

- Transactions demand (∝ Y)
- Precautionary demand (∝ Y)
- Speculative demand (∝ 1/i)

**Liquidity Trap:** When i is very low, money demand infinitely elastic (horizontal LM). Conventional monetary policy ineffective.

### Role of Government

Keynes advocated active fiscal policy to counter recession: increase G, reduce T, run deficit budget.

## Unit 3: IS-LM Model

### IS Curve (Goods Market Equilibrium)

**Derivation:**
```
Y = C + I + G
C = a + b(Y – T)
I = c – d i
G = Ḡ, T = T̄
→ Y = [a + c + Ḡ – bT̄]/(1 – b) – [d/(1 – b)] i
```
- Slope: negative (di/dY = –(1–b)/d)
- Points off IS: right → excess supply; left → excess demand

### LM Curve (Money Market Equilibrium)

**Derivation:**
```
M/P = kY – h i
→ i = (k/h)Y – (1/h)(M/P)
```
- Slope: positive (di/dY = k/h)
- Points off LM: right → excess demand for money; left → excess supply

### Simultaneous Equilibrium

- Intersection of IS and LM gives unique (Y*, i*)
- Adjustment: automatic via changes in Y and i (converges to equilibrium)

### Shifts in Curves

| Shift Factor | IS Curve | LM Curve |
|--------------|----------|----------|
| Increase in G | Right | – |
| Decrease in T | Right | – |
| Increase in M | – | Right |
| Increase in autonomous consumption | Right | – |

### Derivation of AD Curve from IS-LM

- Change in P shifts LM (real money balances change)
- For different P levels, get different Y → downward sloping AD curve

## Unit 4: Open Economy Macroeconomics

### National Income Identity

```
Y = C + I + G + X – M
Y = C + I + G + NX
```

### Balance of Payments

- **Current Account:** Trade balance + net invisibles (services, factor income, transfers)
- **Capital Account:** Capital inflows – outflows (FDI, FII, borrowing)
- **BOP identity:** Current Account + Capital Account + Change in Reserves = 0

### Exchange Rates

- **Nominal exchange rate (e):** units of domestic currency per unit of foreign currency
- **Real exchange rate (r):** r = (e × Pf)/Pd
- **Depreciation:** e increases (rupee weaker)
- **Appreciation:** e decreases (rupee stronger)

### Exchange Rate Regimes

| Regime | Determination | Intervention | Reserves role |
|--------|---------------|--------------|----------------|
| Flexible (float) | Demand & supply | None | No need |
| Fixed | Government/central bank | Active | Essential |
| Managed float | Mostly market, with intervention | Occasional | Important |

### Mundell-Fleming Model (IS-LM-BP)

**Assumptions:**
- Perfect capital mobility → BP horizontal at i = i_f
- Prices fixed (short run)

**Results:**

| Policy | Fixed Exchange Rate | Flexible Exchange Rate |
|--------|---------------------|------------------------|
| Fiscal expansion | Effective (Y↑) | Ineffective (Y unchanged) |
| Monetary expansion | Ineffective (Y unchanged) | Effective (Y↑) |

**Explanation for fiscal policy under flexible rates:**
1. G↑ → IS right → i↑ above i_f
2. Capital inflows → currency appreciates → NX↓ → IS shifts back
3. Returns to original Y, higher i

**Policy Trilemma:** Cannot have all three: fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility, independent monetary policy. Choose any two.

## Unit 5: Consumption Theories

### Keynesian Consumption Function

C = a + bY → APC falls as Y rises (contradicted by Kuznets’ time series data – APC constant)

### Life Cycle Hypothesis (Modigliani & Brumberg)

- Individuals smooth consumption over lifetime.
- Income low when young and old, high in middle years.
- Dissave early, save during working years, dissave after retirement.
- Short-run APC falls with income (cross-section), long-run APC constant (time series) because wealth grows.

**Equation:** C_t = (A₀/T) + (1/T)∑Y_t

### Permanent Income Hypothesis (Friedman)

- Current income = Permanent income (Y^P) + Transitory income (Y^T)
- Consumption depends on Y^P only: C = k Y^P
- Transitory income affects saving.
- Explains cross-section (high Y includes positive transitory → low APC) and time series (transitory averages out).

### Random Walk Hypothesis (Hall)

- Under rational expectations, consumption changes are unpredictable.
- C_t = C_{t-1} + ε_t
- Only unanticipated shocks affect consumption.

## Unit 6: Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans Optimal Growth Model

### Central Planner’s Problem

```
Max W = ∫₀^∞ u(c_t) e^{-ρt} dt
s.t. dk/dt = f(k_t) – n k_t – c_t
k(0) = k₀ given
```

**Hamiltonian:** H = u(c_t)e^{-ρt} + λ_t [f(k_t) – n k_t – c_t]

**First-order conditions:**
1. u'(c_t)e^{-ρt} = λ_t
2. dλ/dt = –λ_t (f'(k_t) – n)
3. dk/dt = f(k_t) – n k_t – c_t
4. Transversality: lim λ_t k_t = 0

**Dynamics:**
- dc/dt = (c_t/σ)(f'(k_t) – n – ρ)   where σ = –(c u''(c))/u'(c)

**Steady State:**
- f'(k*) = n + ρ   (modified golden rule)
- c* = f(k*) – n k*

**Phase Diagram:**
- ȧ = 0 curve: c = f(k) – n k (inverted-U)
- ċ = 0 curve: vertical line at f'(k) = n + ρ
- Unique saddle path converges to steady state.

### Decentralized Household Problem

- Households maximize same utility, face budget constraint: da/dt = w + (r – n)a – c
- Market equilibrium: r = f'(k), w = f(k) – k f'(k), a = k
- Solution identical to central planner’s (welfare theorems hold under perfect foresight).

### Ricardian Equivalence

- Government financing (tax vs. borrowing) does not affect consumption/accumulation.
- Households with perfect foresight anticipate future taxes to repay debt.
- Applies only with lump-sum taxes, no liquidity constraints, infinite horizons.

## Unit 7: Overlapping Generations (OLG) Model

### Structure

- Each generation lives two periods: young (work) and old (retire).
- Population grows at rate n: L_t = (1+n)L_{t-1}
- Utility: U(c_{1t}, c_{2t})

**Budget constraints:**
- Period 1: c_{1t} + s_t = w_t
- Period 2: c_{2t} = (1+r_{t+1}) s_t

**Optimization:** s_t = s(w_t, r_{t+1})

**Goods market equilibrium:**
- Capital next period = savings of current young: K_{t+1} = L_t s_t
- Per capita: k_{t+1} = s(w_t, r_{t+1})/(1+n)

### Dynamic Efficiency

- Golden rule: f'(k_g) = n
- Modified golden rule (Ramsey): f'(k*) = n + ρ
- In OLG, steady state may be dynamically inefficient (k* > k_g) if oversaving occurs.
- Reason: selfish individuals ignore that higher capital reduces returns for future generations.

### Social Security

- **Fully funded:** Government invests contributions → no effect on savings (crowds out private savings one-to-one).
- **Pay-as-you-go:** Transfers from young to old → if r < n, can improve welfare and eliminate dynamic inefficiency.

## Unit 8: Business Cycles

### Features

1. Recurrent expansions and contractions
2. Synchronic across sectors
3. Multidimensional (output, employment, investment, prices)
4. Asymmetric (expansions longer)
5. International contagion

### Phases

```
Peak → Recession → Trough → Recovery → Expansion → Peak
```

### Samuelson’s Multiplier-Accelerator Model

```
Y_t = C_t + I_t
C_t = C_a + c Y_{t-1}
I_t = I_a + v (Y_{t-1} – Y_{t-2})
→ Y_t = C_a + I_a + c Y_{t-1} + v(Y_{t-1} – Y_{t-2})
```

Characteristic equation determines path:
- 0 < c < 1, v small → damped cycles
- v large → explosive cycles
- Certain combinations → constant amplitude cycles

### Economic Indicators

| Type | Examples |
|------|----------|
| Leading | Stock prices, building permits, money supply, consumer sentiment |
| Coincident | GDP, industrial production, employment, personal income |
| Lagging | Unemployment duration, labour costs, inventory levels |

## Unit 9: New Classical & New Keynesian Economics

### New Classical Economics

**Assumptions:**
1. Rational expectations
2. Continuous market clearing (flexible prices/wages)
3. Lucas supply function: y – yⁿ = β(P – Pᵉ)

**Policy Ineffectiveness Proposition (Sargent-Wallace):**
- Only unanticipated monetary policy affects output.
- Systematic policy rules are anticipated and neutral.

**Lucas Critique:** Past statistical relationships break down when policy changes because expectations change.

### New Keynesian Economics

**Features:**
1. Imperfect competition (price-setting firms)
2. Nominal rigidities (menu costs)
3. Real rigidities (labour market, credit market)
4. Microfoundations + rational expectations

**Nominal Rigidities (Menu Costs):**
- Costs of changing prices (printing menus, reprogramming systems) may exceed private benefits.
- Mankiw model: Small menu costs can have large macroeconomic effects because private benefit < social benefit.

**Real Rigidities:**

| Market | Source of Rigidity |
|--------|---------------------|
| Goods | Countercyclical mark-ups, imperfect competition |
| Labour | Efficiency wages, contracting, insider-outsider |
| Credit | Asymmetric information, credit rationing |

**Efficiency Wage Model:**
- Firms pay w > market-clearing to:
  - Reduce shirking (efficiency)
  - Reduce turnover
  - Attract higher ability workers
- Effort function: e = e(w) with e'(w) > 0
- Optimal wage: w e'(w)/e(w) = 1 (elasticity = 1)
- Results in involuntary unemployment.

**Insider-Outsider Model:**
- Insiders (employed) bargain for higher wages.
- Outsiders (unemployed) have no bargaining power.
- Result: wages too high, unemployment persists.

**Search and Matching Model:**
- Labour market frictions: workers search for jobs, firms search for workers.
- Matching function: M = M(U, V)
- Equilibrium unemployment determined by separation rate and hiring cost.

## Unit 10: Monetary Policy

### Objectives

1. Price stability (inflation targeting)
2. Economic growth
3. Exchange rate stability
4. Full employment
5. BOP equilibrium

### Instruments

**Quantitative (affect overall money supply):**
- Bank rate / Repo rate
- Open Market Operations (OMO)
- Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)
- Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)

**Qualitative (selective):**
- Margin requirements
- Credit rationing
- Direct action
- Moral suasion

### Money Supply Measures in India

| Aggregate | Components |
|-----------|------------|
| M0 (Reserve Money) | Currency + Bankers’ deposits with RBI + Other deposits with RBI |
| M1 (Narrow Money) | Currency with public + Demand deposits + Other deposits with RBI |
| M2 | M1 + Post office savings deposits |
| M3 (Broad Money) | M1 + Time deposits with banking system |
| M4 | M3 + Total post office deposits |

### Money Multiplier

**Derivation:**
```
M1 = C + D = (cd + 1)D
M0 = C + R = (cd + rd)D
→ m = M1/M0 = (cd + 1)/(cd + rd)
```
where cd = currency-deposit ratio, rd = reserve-deposit ratio.

**Implications:**
- ΔM1 = m × ΔM0
- Higher cd or rd reduces multiplier

### Transmission Mechanism

Channels:
1. **Interest rate:** M↑ → i↓ → I↑ → AD↑
2. **Credit:** M↑ → bank reserves↑ → loans↑ → I↑
3. **Exchange rate:** M↑ → i↓ → currency depreciates → NX↑
4. **Asset price (Tobin’s q):** M↑ → stock prices↑ → q↑ → I↑
5. **Wealth effect:** M↑ → stock prices↑ → wealth↑ → C↑
6. **Expectations:** M↑ → expected inflation↑ → spending↑

## Unit 11: Fiscal Policy

### Discretionary Fiscal Policy

**Expansionary (to cure recession):**
- Increase G or decrease T
- Leads to budget deficit

**Contractionary (to control inflation):**
- Decrease G or increase T
- Leads to budget surplus

**Time Lags:**
1. Recognition lag (data collection delay)
2. Implementation lag (legislative delay)
3. Response lag (multiplier process)

### Non-Discretionary (Automatic Stabilizers)

- Progressive income taxes: tax revenue falls during recession, cushioning income fall.
- Transfer payments: unemployment benefits rise during recession, supporting demand.
- No legislative action needed; work automatically.

### Fiscal Sustainability

**Government budget constraint:**
```
B_t = (1+r)B_{t-1} + G_t – T_t
```

**Debt-GDP ratio dynamics:**
```
Δ(B/Y) = (r – g)(B_{t-1}/Y_{t-1}) + (G_t – T_t)/Y_t
```

**Sustainability condition:** Primary surplus must be sufficient to stabilize or reduce debt ratio.
- If r > g, debt grows unless primary surplus is positive.
- High debt can lead to debt spiral, higher interest rates, default risk.

**Debt Monetization (Seigniorage):**
- Government finances deficit by printing money.
- Seigniorage = ΔH / P
- Danger: hyperinflation if overused (e.g., Venezuela, Zimbabwe).

## Unit 12: Inflation and Unemployment

### Types of Inflation

- **Demand-pull:** Excess aggregate demand
- **Cost-push:** Rising input costs (wages, oil)
- **Built-in:** Adaptive expectations of inflation

### Types of Unemployment

- **Frictional:** Job search, transitions (short-term)
- **Structural:** Mismatch of skills/location
- **Cyclical:** Due to business cycle

### Natural Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU)

- Unemployment at which inflation is stable (non-accelerating).
- Friedman-Phelps: natural rate determined by real factors (frictions, institutions).
- Long-run Phillips curve vertical at uⁿ.

### Phillips Curve

**Original (Phillips, 1958):**
```
ẇ = a – b u   (wage inflation vs. unemployment)
```

**Expectations-Augmented:**
```
π = πᵉ – β(u – uⁿ)
```

**Adaptive Expectations:**
```
π = π₋₁ – β(u – uⁿ)
```
- Short-run trade-off exists.
- But in long run, π = πᵉ → u = uⁿ.

**Rational Expectations:**
- No trade-off even in short run if policy is anticipated.
- Only surprises affect output.

### Stagflation

- High inflation + high unemployment (1970s oil crises).
- Explained by supply shocks shifting Phillips curve outward.

## Question 1: Classical vs. Keynesian Theory (20 marks)

**Q:** *Compare and contrast the Classical and Keynesian theories of output and employment determination. Use appropriate diagrams. Examine the relevance of Classical features in the present context.*

### Answer

#### Introduction

Macroeconomic theory has evolved through two major schools: Classical (18th-19th century) and Keynesian (1936 onward). Their fundamental disagreement lies in the assumptions about price flexibility and the role of aggregate demand.

#### Classical Theory

**Core Assumptions:**
1. Perfect competition in all markets
2. Fully flexible wages and prices
3. Say’s Law: Supply creates its own demand
4. Money is neutral (affects only prices)
5. Classical dichotomy: real and monetary sectors separate

**Determination of Output and Employment:**

*Labour Market (Fig. 1):*
```
Real wage (W/P)
↑
   Ns (upward sloping)
   |
   |   Equilibrium at E: N = Nf, W/P = (W/P)*
   |  /
   | /
   |/______ Nd (downward sloping, MPN)
   +----------------→ N
        Nf
```

*Production Function:*
```
Y
↑
   |               Y = F(K, N)
   |              /
   |             /
   |            /
   |___________/________→ N
               Nf
   Yf ────────┘
```

At full employment Nf, output Yf (potential output).

*Aggregate Supply (Fig. 2):*
```
P
↑
   | AS (vertical at Yf)
   |    |
   |    |
   |    |
   +----+--------→ Y
        Yf
```

Any increase in AD (e.g., from monetary expansion) raises only prices, not output.

**Quantity Theory of Money:**
```
MV = PY
```
V constant, Y fixed at Yf ⇒ P ∝ M. Money is neutral.

**Say’s Law:**
Production generates income equal to value of output. Savings are matched by investment via flexible interest rates. No general overproduction.

#### Keynesian Theory

**Core Assumptions:**
1. Imperfect competition
2. Rigid (sticky) wages and prices, especially downward
3. Demand determines output (effective demand principle)
4. Money can affect real variables in short run

**Determination of Output (Two-sector model):**

```
Aggregate Expenditure
↑
   | 45° line (Y = AE)
   |    AE = C + I
   |   /
   |  / E
   | /   \
   |/     \
   +-------→ Y
     Y₀   Yf
```

- At E, Y₀ < Yf (recessionary gap)
- Government can increase G or reduce T to shift AE up to full employment.

**Multiplier Effect:**
```
ΔY = (1/(1-MPC)) × ΔI
```
Example: MPC = 0.8, multiplier = 5. A ₹100 crore investment increases output by ₹500 crore.

**Liquidity Preference:**
- Money demand: L = kY – hi
- Increase in M reduces i, stimulates I, raises Y.

#### Diagrammatic Comparison

| Aspect | Classical | Keynesian |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| AS curve | Vertical | Horizontal (up to full employment) |
| AD shifts | Only P changes | Y changes |
| Unemployment | Voluntary | Involuntary possible |
| Policy role | None (laissez-faire) | Active (fiscal & monetary) |

**Figure: Classical vs. Keynesian AS-AD**

```
Classical:                          Keynesian:
P                                   P
↑                                   ↑
| AS                                | AS (horizontal)
|   |                               |___________
|   |                               |          |
|   | AD1                           |    AD1   |
|   |  AD0                          |   AD0    |
|   |   |                           |   |      |
|   |   |                           |   |      |
|   |   |                           |   |      |
|   +---+---→ Y                     +---+---→ Y
        Yf                              Y0 Yf
```

#### Relevance of Classical Features Today

**Still Valid:**
1. **Quantity Theory:** In long run, sustained money growth causes inflation (e.g., 1970s, 2021-22).
2. **Supply-side matters:** Productivity, technology, capital accumulation drive growth.
3. **Market efficiency:** Many markets (e.g., agricultural commodities) clear via prices.
4. **Natural rate hypothesis:** Cannot keep unemployment below natural rate permanently without accelerating inflation (Volcker disinflation, 1980s).

**Not Valid:**
1. **Great Depression (1929-33):** Markets did not self-correct; unemployment 25% in US.
2. **Global Financial Crisis (2008):** Required massive government intervention (stimulus, bank bailouts).
3. **COVID recession (2020):** Active fiscal and monetary policy prevented collapse.
4. **Price stickiness:** Empirical evidence shows wages and prices adjust slowly (menu costs, contracts).

#### Conclusion

Classical theory explains long-run equilibrium and inflation dynamics. Keynesian theory is essential for short-run stabilization. Modern macroeconomics (neoclassical synthesis) combines both: Keynesian in short run, Classical in long run.

## Question 2: IS-LM Model – Derivation, Equilibrium, and Adjustment (20 marks)

**Q:** *Derive the IS and LM curves. Explain how simultaneous equilibrium is achieved in the real and monetary sectors. What happens if the economy is at a point off the curves? Does it automatically converge?*

### Answer

#### Introduction

The IS-LM model, developed by Hicks and Hansen, represents the Keynesian system in a general equilibrium framework. IS stands for Investment-Saving (goods market equilibrium), LM for Liquidity preference-Money supply (money market equilibrium).

#### Derivation of IS Curve

**Goods market equilibrium condition:** Y = C + I + G

**Specification:**
```
C = a + b(Y – T)      (a > 0, 0 < b < 1)
I = c – d i           (c > 0, d > 0)
G = Ḡ (exogenous), T = T̄ (lump sum)
```

**Substituting:**
```
Y = a + b(Y – T̄) + c – d i + Ḡ
Y – bY = a – bT̄ + c + Ḡ – d i
Y(1 – b) = (a + c + Ḡ – bT̄) – d i
```

**IS equation:**
```
Y = [a + c + Ḡ – bT̄]/(1 – b)  –  [d/(1 – b)] i
```

**Properties:**
- Slope: di/dY = –(1 – b)/d  (negative)
- Intercept (i=0): Y = (a + c + Ḡ – bT̄)/(1 – b)
- Steeper IS: larger (1 – b)/d (low MPC or low interest sensitivity)

**Points off IS:**
- To the right of IS: excess supply of goods (inventory accumulation) → Y↓
- To the left of IS: excess demand for goods → Y↑

#### Derivation of LM Curve

**Money market equilibrium:** M/P = L = kY – h i

**Specification:**
```
M = exogenous money supply (controlled by central bank)
P = fixed price level (short run)
k > 0 (income elasticity of money demand)
h > 0 (interest sensitivity of money demand)
```

**Solving for i:**
```
kY – h i = M/P
– h i = M/P – kY
i = (k/h)Y – (1/h)(M/P)
```

**LM equation:**
```
i = (k/h)Y – (1/h)(M/P)
```

**Properties:**
- Slope: di/dY = k/h > 0 (positive)
- Intercept (Y=0): i = –(1/h)(M/P) (negative, not economically meaningful)
- Steeper LM: larger k/h (high income sensitivity or low interest sensitivity of money demand)

**Points off LM:**
- To the right of LM: excess demand for money (sell bonds) → i↑
- To the left of LM: excess supply of money (buy bonds) → i↓

#### Simultaneous Equilibrium

**Figure: IS-LM Equilibrium**

```
i
↑
   LM
   \
    \  E (i*, Y*)
     \ |
      \|
       +-------- IS
        \
         \
          +→ Y
```

At point E, both markets clear: goods market (Y = C+I+G) and money market (M/P = L).

**Adjustment Dynamics (Convergence):**

The economy automatically converges to equilibrium through the following process:

| Region | Goods Market | Money Market | Adjustment |
|--------|--------------|--------------|------------|
| I (right of IS, above LM) | Excess supply | Excess demand | Y↓, i↑ |
| II (right of IS, below LM) | Excess supply | Excess supply | Y↓, i↓ |
| III (left of IS, below LM) | Excess demand | Excess supply | Y↑, i↓ |
| IV (left of IS, above LM) | Excess demand | Excess demand | Y↑, i↑ |

**Example of convergence from point A (above LM, right of IS):**

```
i
↑
   LM
   \  A
    \   \
     \    \
      E    \
       \    \
        +----+---- IS
            B
```

- At A: Excess supply of goods → firms reduce production (Y↓)
- At A: Excess demand for money → sell bonds → i↑
- Movement towards E along a path.

**Why convergence is stable:**
- Changes in output respond to inventory accumulation/depletion.
- Changes in interest rates respond to bond market pressures.
- The system has a unique stable equilibrium (saddle point property in continuous time; in discrete time with plausible parameters, convergence is monotonic or oscillatory but stable).

**Mathematical proof (simple):** The system can be written as:
```
Y = α (A – d i)
i = (k/h)Y – (1/h)(M/P)
```
Substituting gives a first-order difference/differential equation with coefficient < 1 in absolute value → convergence.

#### Shocks and Adjustments

**Monetary expansion (M↑):**
- LM shifts right → i↓, Y↑
- New equilibrium at higher Y, lower i

**Fiscal expansion (G↑ or T↓):**
- IS shifts right → i↑, Y↑
- New equilibrium at higher Y, higher i (crowding out partial)

#### Conclusion

The IS-LM model shows that the economy automatically converges to a unique equilibrium where goods and money markets clear. The speed and path depend on parameters, but stability is assured. Points off the curves generate forces (changes in Y and i) that move the economy toward intersection.

## Question 3: Real Business Cycle Theory – Causes and Simple Model (20 marks)

**Q:** *What according to the Real Business Cycle (RBC) theory are the reasons for business cycles in an economy? Describe a simple RBC model. How does it explain fluctuations?*

### Answer

#### Introduction

Real Business Cycle theory, pioneered by Kydland and Prescott (1982), explains business cycles as the optimal response of rational agents to real (not monetary) shocks, particularly productivity (technology) shocks. It integrates growth theory and cycle theory within a single framework.

#### Causes of Business Cycles According to RBC

**Primary cause:** **Technology (productivity) shocks** – unexpected changes in the production function.

**Types of real shocks:**
1. **Supply shocks:** Changes in productivity (new inventions, innovations, learning-by-doing)
2. **Resource shocks:** Oil price changes, raw material availability
3. **Regulatory shocks:** Changes in taxes, environmental regulations
4. **Natural shocks:** Weather, earthquakes, pandemics
5. **Fiscal shocks:** Changes in government purchases (real, not nominal)

**Key insight:** Monetary shocks are *not* the primary cause. Money is neutral or only a minor factor.

#### Assumptions of RBC Theory

1. **Rational expectations** – Agents optimize using all available information.
2. **Continuous market clearing** – Prices and wages are perfectly flexible; markets always clear.
3. **No distinction between short run and long run** – Same model explains both growth and cycles.
4. **Real factors drive cycles** – Technology shocks are the main impulse.
5. **Intertemporal substitution** – Workers adjust labour supply based on current vs. future real wages.

#### A Simple RBC Model

**1. Production function (Cobb-Douglas with technology shock):**
```
Y_t = A_t K_t^α (L_t)^{1-α}
```
where A_t is the stochastic technology level.

**2. Technology shock process (AR(1)):**
```
ln A_t = (1-ρ) ln A + ρ ln A_{t-1} + ε_t
```
- ρ ∈ (0,1) measures persistence
- ε_t ~ white noise (zero mean, constant variance)
- A = steady-state technology

**3. Household utility (intertemporal):**
```
U = E₀ Σ β^t [ln C_t + θ ln(1 – L_t)]
```
- β = discount factor
- θ = preference for leisure

**4. Resource constraint:**
```
C_t + I_t = Y_t
K_{t+1} = (1-δ)K_t + I_t
```

**5. Optimality conditions (from household maximization):**

*Labour-leisure trade-off:*
```
θ C_t / (1 – L_t) = (1-α) Y_t / L_t
```
(MRS between consumption and leisure = real wage)

*Euler equation (consumption smoothing):*
```
1/C_t = β E_t[(1-α)Y_{t+1}/K_{t+1} + (1-δ)] / C_{t+1}
```

**6. Equilibrium:** The system of equations solves for {C_t, L_t, K_{t+1}, Y_t} given shocks.

#### How RBC Explains Fluctuations

**Positive technology shock (A_t ↑):**

*Step 1: Immediate effect*
- Production function shifts up → higher MPK and MPL
- Higher real wage → workers increase labour supply (substitution effect dominates income effect)
- Higher output Y↑

*Step 2: Consumption and investment*
- Households anticipate higher future income → consume more today
- Higher MPK → firms invest more → capital accumulates
- Both C and I increase

*Step 3: Propagation*
- Higher capital next period → further output increase
- Persistent shock (ρ close to 1) → prolonged boom
- Employment is pro-cyclical (rises during booms)

**Negative technology shock:**
- Opposite effects: output falls, employment falls, consumption and investment fall.
- Recession.

**Figure: Response to a Positive Technology Shock**

```
Output (Y)        Employment (L)       Real wage (W/P)
↑                 ↑                    ↑
|   /‾‾‾‾‾‾        |   /‾‾‾‾‾‾           |   /‾‾‾‾‾‾
|  /               |  /                 |  /
| /                | /                  | /
|/                 |/                   |/
+──→ time          +──→ time            +──→ time
```

**Pro-cyclical real wages:** RBC predicts real wages rise in booms (consistent with data), unlike simple Keynesian models with fixed nominal wages.

#### Intertemporal Substitution of Labour

A key mechanism: workers shift labour supply across time in response to temporary wage changes.

**Two-period example:**
```
U = ln C₁ + θ ln(1-L₁) + β[ln C₂ + θ ln(1-L₂)]
Budget: C₁ + C₂/(1+r) = w₁ L₁ + w₂ L₂/(1+r)
```
Optimal condition:
```
(1-L₁)/(1-L₂) = (w₂/w₁) × [1/(β(1+r))]
```
If w₁ rises temporarily (relative to w₂), L₁ increases (work more today).

This explains why employment fluctuates without requiring “fooling” or price surprises.

#### Empirical Implications

**Stylized facts explained by RBC:**
1. Output, consumption, investment, hours worked are pro-cyclical and positively correlated.
2. Real wages are moderately pro-cyclical.
3. Investment is more volatile than output.
4. Productivity (Solow residual) is pro-cyclical.

**Criticisms of RBC:**
1. Technology shocks are not large enough to explain observed fluctuations (the “productivity puzzle”).
2. Monetary shocks do affect output (evidence from Fed policy).
3. Labour supply is not that elastic intertemporally (micro evidence).
4. Does not explain periods of high unemployment without technology regress (Great Depression).

#### Conclusion

RBC theory provides a coherent, micro-founded explanation of business cycles as efficient responses to real shocks. While controversial, it has profoundly influenced modern macroeconomics by emphasizing general equilibrium, microfoundations, and the importance of supply shocks.

## Question 4: Nominal vs. Real Rigidities – Types and Significance (20 marks)

**Q:** *Distinguish between nominal rigidities and real rigidities. Give a brief description of the types of real rigidities in an economy. Why are they important for New Keynesian economics?*

### Answer

#### Introduction

Rigidities (stickiness) in prices and wages are central to Keynesian economics. New Keynesians distinguish between **nominal rigidities** (prices/wages don't adjust in money terms) and **real rigidities** (relative prices/wages don't adjust even if flexible in nominal terms).

#### Nominal Rigidities

**Definition:** Inflexibility of prices and wages expressed in monetary units (rupees, dollars). They fail to change in response to macroeconomic shocks.

**Examples:**
- Sticky prices (menu costs)
- Sticky wages (long-term contracts)
- Money illusion

**Key concept – Menu Costs:**
- Costs of changing prices: printing new menus, reprogramming computers, informing customers.
- Even if small (e.g., 0.1% of revenue), they may exceed the private benefit of price adjustment.
- Mankiw (1985): Small menu costs can cause large macroeconomic fluctuations because private benefit < social benefit.

**Diagram: Menu Cost Model**

```
Profit (π)
↑
   |        π(p)
   |       /‾‾‾\
   |      /     \
   |     /       \
   |    /         \
   |   /           \
   |  /             \
   | /               \
   |/                 \
   +-------------------→ Price (p)
      p*    p₀
```
- Optimal price p* yields max profit.
- If actual price p₀ ≠ p*, profit loss is small (second-order) when close to optimum.
- Menu cost may exceed profit loss → firm doesn't adjust.
- But social cost (unemployment) can be large.

#### Real Rigidities

**Definition:** Factors that prevent relative prices (e.g., real wage, mark-up) from adjusting to clear markets, even if nominal prices are flexible.

**Types of Real Rigidities:**

### 1. Real Rigidities in the Goods Market

**Source:** Imperfect competition and countercyclical mark-ups.

**Mechanism:**
- Firms set price as mark-up over marginal cost: P = μ × MC
- In perfect competition, μ = 1. In imperfect competition, μ > 1.
- If μ falls during booms (more competition), prices rise less than costs → output adjusts.

**Why mark-ups are countercyclical:**
- **Thick-market effects:** More firms enter during booms, increasing competition.
- **Collusion breakdown:** High profits induce cheating on oligopoly agreements.
- **Customer search:** During booms, customers search less, but firms compete more.

**Implication:** Demand shifts affect quantity more than price.

### 2. Real Rigidities in the Labour Market

**Sources:** Efficiency wages, insider-outsider bargaining, contracting.

**Efficiency Wage Model:**
- Firms pay w > market-clearing to increase productivity.
- Effort function: e = e(w) with e'(w) > 0.
- Optimal condition: w e'(w)/e(w) = 1 (elasticity of effort w.r.t. wage = 1).
- Result: Real wage is rigid (determined by effort function, not demand).
- Unemployment persists.

**Insider-Outsider Model:**
- Insiders (employed workers) bargain for high wages.
- Outsiders (unemployed) have no bargaining power.
- Wages set above market-clearing → unemployment.

**Contracting Models:**
- Long-term explicit or implicit contracts fix wages for several periods.
- Wages do not respond to current labour market conditions.

### 3. Real Rigidities in the Credit Market

**Source:** Asymmetric information between lenders and borrowers.

**Problems:**
- **Adverse selection:** Borrowers with riskiest projects seek loans most aggressively.
- **Moral hazard:** Borrowers may take excessive risks after receiving loans.
- **Credit rationing:** Lenders limit loan quantity rather than raise interest rates (Stiglitz-Weiss).

**Consequences:**
- Monetary policy affects economy through **credit channel** (quantity of loans) as much as interest rate channel.
- Tight money → bank reserves↓ → loans↓ → investment↓ → output↓.
- Credit market imperfections amplify and propagate shocks.

#### Importance for New Keynesian Economics

1. **Explain involuntary unemployment:** Real rigidities (efficiency wages, insider-outsider) show why wages don't fall to clear market even if nominal wages are flexible.

2. **Amplify nominal rigidities:** With real rigidities, small nominal rigidities have large macroeconomic effects (Ball & Romer, 1990). If real wages are rigid, price stickiness causes large output fluctuations.

3. **Provide microfoundations:** Real rigidities are derived from optimizing behaviour (effort function, asymmetric information), addressing Lucas's critique of Keynesianism.

4. **Explain stylized facts:** Pro-cyclical real wages, countercyclical mark-ups, credit crunches.

5. **Policy implications:** Active policy can improve welfare because market imperfections prevent efficient adjustment.

#### Comparison Table

| Feature | Nominal Rigidities | Real Rigidities |
|---------|---------------------|-----------------|
| Nature | Prices/wages fixed in money terms | Relative prices/wages unresponsive |
| Cause | Menu costs, contracts | Imperfect competition, asymmetric info |
| Example | Sticky price of newspaper | Efficiency wage above market-clearing |
| Persistence | Short-run (weeks to months) | Long-run (can persist indefinitely) |
| Policy relevance | Monetary policy non-neutral | Fiscal & structural policy needed |

#### Conclusion

Both nominal and real rigidities are essential for New Keynesian models to generate realistic business cycle dynamics. Nominal rigidities allow monetary policy to have real effects; real rigidities make these effects persistent and large. The combination explains why markets do not clear instantly and why stabilization policy remains relevant.

## Question 5: Dynamic Inconsistency with Loss Function (12 marks)

**Q:** *Explain the concept of dynamic inconsistency by using a loss function.*

### Answer

#### Definition

Dynamic inconsistency (time inconsistency) occurs when a policymaker has an incentive to deviate from a previously announced policy once expectations are formed, even though no new information has arrived. This undermines credibility.

#### The Basic Model (Barro-Gordon)

**Loss function of the Central Bank:**
```
L = π² + (y – yˢ)²
```
where:
- π = inflation rate
- y = output
- yˢ = output target ( > yⁿ, the natural rate)
- The CB wants to minimize L (zero inflation and output at target is “bliss point”).

**Phillips Curve constraint:**
```
π = πᵉ + β(y – yⁿ)
```
- β > 0
- πᵉ = expected inflation (formed before CB sets π)

#### Step-by-Step Derivation

**1. Discretionary policy (no commitment):**

CB chooses π to minimize L given πᵉ and y.

Substitute Phillips Curve into loss function:
```
L = π² + [yⁿ + (π – πᵉ)/β – yˢ]²
```
First-order condition:
```
∂L/∂π = 2π + 2[yⁿ + (π – πᵉ)/β – yˢ] × (1/β) = 0
```
Simplify:
```
π + (1/β²)(π – πᵉ) + (1/β)(yⁿ – yˢ) = 0
```
Multiply by β²:
```
β²π + (π – πᵉ) + β(yⁿ – yˢ) = 0
(β² + 1)π = πᵉ – β(yⁿ – yˢ)
π = [πᵉ – β(yⁿ – yˢ)] / (1 + β²)
```

Now impose rational expectations: πᵉ = E(π) = π (average).

So:
```
π = [π – β(yⁿ – yˢ)] / (1 + β²)
π(1 + β²) = π – β(yⁿ – yˢ)
πβ² = –β(yⁿ – yˢ)
π = –(yⁿ – yˢ)/β = (yˢ – yⁿ)/β
```
Since yˢ > yⁿ, π > 0.

**Outcome under discretion:**
```
π_d = (yˢ – yⁿ)/β
y_d = yⁿ
Loss_d = π_d² + 0 = [(yˢ – yⁿ)/β]²
```

**2. Rule (commitment to zero inflation):**

CB announces π = 0. If credible, πᵉ = 0.
From Phillips Curve: 0 = 0 + β(y – yⁿ) ⇒ y = yⁿ.

**Outcome under rule:**
```
π_r = 0
y_r = yⁿ
Loss_r = 0
```

**3. The inconsistency:**

If CB announces π=0 and public believes it (πᵉ=0), the CB faces the same Phillips Curve: π = β(y – yⁿ).

From the loss function, given πᵉ=0, what π should CB choose?

Using the same FOC with πᵉ=0:
```
π = [0 – β(yⁿ – yˢ)] / (1 + β²) = β(yˢ – yⁿ)/(1 + β²) > 0
```
This is positive! The CB can get y > yⁿ by creating surprise inflation.

**Cheating outcome:**
```
π_c = β(yˢ – yⁿ)/(1 + β²)  (smaller than π_d)
y_c = yⁿ + (π_c – 0)/β = yⁿ + (yˢ – yⁿ)/(1 + β²) > yⁿ
Loss_c = π_c² + (y_c – yˢ)² < Loss_d (since closer to bliss)
```

**So:** Even though the rule gives lower loss (0), the CB has an incentive to cheat once expectations are fixed. The public anticipates this, so the rule is not credible. The only credible equilibrium is discretion, with positive inflation.

#### Diagram

```
π
↑
   U₀ (loss=0)        U₁ (loss>0)
   |                  |
   |  Bliss (π=0, y=yˢ)  |
   |    *              |
   |    |\             |
   |    | \            |
   |    |  \           |
   |    |   \          |
   |    |    \         |
   |    |     \        |
   |    |      \       |
   |    |       \      |
   |    |        \     |
   |    |         \    |
   |    |          \   |
   |    |           \  |
   |    |            \ |
   |    |             \|
   +----+-------------+→ y
       yⁿ           yˢ
```

- The Phillips Curve (PC) is upward sloping.
- The indifference curves (loss) are circles around bliss point.
- Under discretion, equilibrium at intersection of PC and vertical line through yⁿ? Actually at tangency, giving π>0.
- Under rule, at point (yⁿ, 0) but not on PC (inconsistent) unless πᵉ=0 and CB doesn't cheat.

#### Solution to Dynamic Inconsistency

1. **Conservative Central Banker (Rogoff):** Appoint a CB who hates inflation more than output (γ > 1 in loss function: L = γπ² + (y–yˢ)²). This reduces inflation bias.

2. **Contracts and incentives:** Tie CB's compensation to inflation performance.

3. **Reputation:** In repeated games, a CB can build reputation for low inflation, sustaining the rule.

4. **Inflation targeting:** Explicit numerical target increases accountability and credibility.

#### Conclusion

Dynamic inconsistency explains why discretionary monetary policy leads to an inflation bias (too high inflation) without output gains. It justifies institutional reforms like independent central banks with clear mandates.

## Question 6: Money Multiplier – Derivation and Implications (12 marks)

**Q:** *Explain the concept of money multiplier and derive it in terms of currency-deposit ratio and reserve-deposit ratio. What are its implications for monetary policy?*

### Answer

#### Definition

The money multiplier (m) is the ratio of money supply (M1) to the monetary base (M0). It indicates how much the money supply increases for each rupee increase in the monetary base.

```
m = M1 / M0
```

#### Derivation

**Notation:**
- C = currency held by public
- D = demand deposits
- R = bank reserves (vault cash + deposits with RBI)
- cd = C/D = currency-deposit ratio (chosen by public)
- rd = R/D = reserve-deposit ratio (determined by CRR + excess reserves)

**Monetary base (high-powered money, M0):**
```
M0 = C + R
```
All currency (whether held by public or banks) plus bank reserves.

**Money supply (M1):**
```
M1 = C + D
```

**Step 1: Express in terms of D and ratios**
```
C = cd × D
R = rd × D
```

**Step 2: M0 in terms of D**
```
M0 = cd D + rd D = (cd + rd) D
⇒ D = M0 / (cd + rd)
```

**Step 3: M1 in terms of D**
```
M1 = cd D + D = (cd + 1) D
```

**Step 4: Substitute D**
```
M1 = (cd + 1) × [M0 / (cd + rd)]
```

**Step 5: Money multiplier**
```
m = M1 / M0 = (cd + 1) / (cd + rd)
```

#### Numerical Example

Suppose:
- cd = 0.4 (public holds 40% of deposits as cash)
- rd = 0.1 (banks keep 10% reserves)

Then:
```
m = (0.4 + 1) / (0.4 + 0.1) = 1.4 / 0.5 = 2.8
```

If RBI increases M0 by ₹100 crore, M1 increases by ₹280 crore.

#### Factors Affecting the Multiplier

| Factor | Change | Effect on m | Reason |
|--------|--------|-------------|--------|
| cd ↑ | Public holds more cash | m ↓ | Less deposit creation |
| rd ↑ | Higher CRR or excess reserves | m ↓ | Banks lend less |
| cd ↓ | Public prefers deposits | m ↑ | More deposit expansion |
| rd ↓ | Lower reserve requirements | m ↑ | More lending capacity |

#### Implications for Monetary Policy

1. **Control of money supply:** RBI can control M1 by changing M0 (via OMO) or influencing cd and rd (via CRR, SLR, interest rates).

2. **Predictability:** If cd and rd are stable, m is stable, and RBI can target money supply. If unstable, money targeting is difficult.

3. **Crisis periods:** During financial crises (e.g., 2008), cd rises (bank runs) and rd rises (banks hoard reserves) → m falls sharply → money supply contracts even if M0 expands.

**Example – Great Depression in US:**
- 1929-33: M0 increased slightly, but M1 fell by 30% because m collapsed from ~3 to ~1.5.
- Caused by bank failures → cd↑, rd↑.

4. **Policy levers:**
   - **CRR change:** rd ↑ → m ↓ → M1 ↓ (contractionary)
   - **OMO:** M0 ↑ → M1 ↑ by m times (expansionary)
   - **Interest on reserves:** Higher interest on reserves → rd ↑ → m ↓

#### Money Multiplier in India

Current approximate values (2024):
- cd ≈ 0.45 (currency in circulation ~ ₹34 lakh crore, deposits ~ ₹75 lakh crore)
- rd = CRR (4.5%) + excess reserves (≈ 2%) ≈ 0.065
- m = (1.45)/(0.515) ≈ 2.82

M0 ≈ ₹45 lakh crore, M3 ≈ ₹150 lakh crore → m ≈ 3.33 (including time deposits).

#### Limitations

1. **Endogenous money theory (Post-Keynesian):** Banks create deposits when loans are demanded; the multiplier operates in reverse (loans create deposits, not reserves create loans). Horizontalists argue m is not stable.

2. **Excess reserves:** Banks may not lend all excess reserves, especially during recessions (liquidity trap).

3. **Currency leakage:** High cd reduces multiplier.

#### Conclusion

The money multiplier is a useful tool for understanding how changes in the monetary base translate into money supply. While its stability is debated, it remains central to textbook monetary policy analysis and central bank operations.

## Question 7: Business Cycles – Samuelson Model (12 marks)

**Q:** *What is meant by business cycle? What are its important features? Discuss Samuelson's model of business cycle.*

### Answer

#### Definition

A business cycle refers to the recurrent but irregular fluctuations in aggregate economic activity (output, employment, income, sales) around its long-term growth trend.

#### Important Features

1. **Recurrence but not periodic:** Cycles occur regularly but not at fixed intervals (duration 2-12 years).

2. **Synchronicity:** Expansions and contractions occur across most sectors simultaneously.

3. **Co-movement:** Macro variables move together – output, consumption, investment, employment are pro-cyclical; unemployment is counter-cyclical.

4. **Asymmetry:** Expansions are typically longer and milder; contractions are shorter and sharper (except Great Depression).

5. **Persistence:** Shocks have lasting effects (output remains above or below trend for several periods).

6. **International transmission:** Cycles spread across countries via trade and finance.

#### Phases of Business Cycle

```
Economic Activity
↑
   |     Peak
   |    /‾‾‾‾‾\
   |   /       \  Recession
   |  /         \
   | /           \    Trough
   |/             \  /‾‾‾‾‾\
   +----------------+------+-------→ Time
        Expansion      Recovery
```

- **Expansion:** Output rising, employment increasing, confidence improving.
- **Peak:** Maximum output, turning point.
- **Recession:** Output falling, unemployment rising, investment falling.
- **Trough:** Minimum output, turning point.
- **Recovery:** Output rising from trough.

#### Samuelson’s Multiplier-Accelerator Model

**Purpose:** To explain how internal dynamics (interaction of multiplier and accelerator) can generate endogenous cycles without external shocks.

**Assumptions:**
1. Consumption depends on previous period’s income (lagged):
   ```
   C_t = C_a + c Y_{t-1}
   ```
   where c = MPC (0 < c < 1)

2. Induced investment depends on change in income (accelerator principle):
   ```
   I_t = I_a + v (Y_{t-1} – Y_{t-2})
   ```
   where v = accelerator coefficient (capital-output ratio, v > 0)

3. Government spending and autonomous investment are constant (ignored or treated as exogenous).

**Equilibrium condition:** Y_t = C_t + I_t

**Substituting:**
```
Y_t = C_a + c Y_{t-1} + I_a + v(Y_{t-1} – Y_{t-2})
Y_t = (C_a + I_a) + (c + v)Y_{t-1} – v Y_{t-2}
```

Let A = C_a + I_a (autonomous spending). Then:
```
Y_t – (c + v)Y_{t-1} + v Y_{t-2} = A
```

**Second-order linear difference equation.**

**Characteristic equation:**
```
λ² – (c + v)λ + v = 0
```

**Roots:**
```
λ = [ (c+v) ± √((c+v)² – 4v) ] / 2
```

**Solution paths depend on c and v:**

| Region | Condition | Type of Path |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| A | (c+v)² < 4v | Damped oscillations (stable) |
| B | (c+v)² > 4v, c+v < 1 | Monotonic convergence |
| C | (c+v)² > 4v, c+v > 1 | Monotonic divergence |
| D | (c+v)² < 4v, c+v > 1 | Explosive oscillations |
| E | (c+v)² = 4v, c+v = 1 | Constant amplitude cycles (borderline) |

**Graphical representation:**

```
c (MPC)
↑
1.0 |                 |
    |                 |
    |        D        |  C
    |    (explosive   | (monotonic
    |     cycles)     |  divergence)
    |                 |
0.5 |                 |
    |        A        |  B
    |    (damped      | (monotonic
    |     cycles)     | convergence)
    |                 |
0   +-----+-----+-----+→ v
    0    1     2     3
```

**Interpretation:**
- **Region A (low c, low v):** Damped cycles – economy returns to equilibrium after a few oscillations.
- **Region D (high c, high v):** Explosive cycles – cycles grow in amplitude (requires non-linear constraints to limit amplitude).
- **Region B:** Smooth adjustment without cycles.
- **Boundary E (c+v=1, v=1/4):** Constant amplitude (Hicks’ trade cycle).

#### Example

Let c = 0.6, v = 0.5 (region A: (1.1)² = 1.21 < 4×0.5=2). Suppose a shock increases autonomous spending by 1 unit.

Initial conditions: Y_0 = 100, Y_1 = 100 (equilibrium).

After shock, A increases from 100 to 101 (or add 1 to Y equation). Compute:

```
Y₂ = 101 + 1.1×100 – 0.5×100 = 101 + 110 – 50 = 161
Y₃ = 101 + 1.1×161 – 0.5×100 = 101 + 177.1 – 50 = 228.1
Y₄ = 101 + 1.1×228.1 – 0.5×161 = 101 + 250.9 – 80.5 = 271.4
...
```
Eventually, cycles dampen and converge to new equilibrium Y = 101/(1-c) = 101/0.4 = 252.5.

#### Diagram of Damped Cycles

```
Y
↑
   |  /\    /\
   | /  \  /  \
   |/    \/    \________
   +----------------→ t
```

#### Limitations of Samuelson Model

1. **No external shocks:** Requires perfect tuning of parameters to get cycles; most economies are not purely endogenous.
2. **Fixed accelerator:** In reality, v is not constant; investment depends on expected future demand.
3. **No capacity constraints:** Model allows output to rise indefinitely; in reality, full employment limits expansion.
4. **No price mechanism:** Assumes fixed prices; inflation ignored.
5. **Lag structure arbitrary:** Why two-period lag? Could be other specifications.

#### Contribution

Despite limitations, Samuelson’s model was the first to show mathematically how the interaction of multiplier and accelerator can generate cyclical behaviour. It laid the foundation for modern dynamic macroeconomics.

# PART 4: PRACTICE PROBLEMS WITH SOLUTIONS

## Problem 1: Keynesian Equilibrium

**Given:** C = 200 + 0.75Y, I = 100, G = 150, T = 100 (lump sum). Find equilibrium Y. If full employment Yf = 2000, what policy change is needed?

**Solution:**
```
Y = C + I + G
Y = 200 + 0.75(Y – 100) + 100 + 150
Y = 200 + 0.75Y – 75 + 250
Y – 0.75Y = 375
0.25Y = 375
Y = 1500
```

Recessionary gap = 500.

Multiplier = 1/(1-0.75) = 4.

To increase Y by 500, need ΔG = 500/4 = 125.

Or ΔT = –500 × (0.75/0.25) = –1500 (not practical).

## Problem 2: IS-LM Equilibrium

**Given:** C = 100 + 0.8(Y – T), I = 200 – 20i, G = 200, T = 100, M/P = 400, L = 0.4Y – 10i. Find Y and i.

**Solution:**
IS: Y = 100 + 0.8(Y – 100) + 200 – 20i + 200
Y = 100 + 0.8Y – 80 + 400 – 20i
Y – 0.8Y = 420 – 20i
0.2Y = 420 – 20i
Y = 2100 – 100i   ...(1)

LM: 400 = 0.4Y – 10i
0.4Y = 400 + 10i
Y = 1000 + 25i   ...(2)

Equate: 2100 – 100i = 1000 + 25i
1100 = 125i
i = 8.8%

Y = 1000 + 25×8.8 = 1000 + 220 = 1220.

## Problem 3: Money Multiplier

**Given:** cd = 0.3, rd = 0.08. Calculate m. If RBI increases M0 by ₹50 crore, what is ΔM1? If cd rises to 0.4, what happens to m?

**Solution:**
m = (0.3+1)/(0.3+0.08) = 1.3/0.38 = 3.421

ΔM1 = 3.421 × 50 = ₹171.05 crore

If cd = 0.4: m = 1.4/0.48 = 2.917 (decrease)

## Problem 4: Phillips Curve

**Given:** π = πᵉ – 0.4(u – 0.05). If πᵉ = 0.03 and actual π = 0.05, find u. If the government wants u = 0.04, what π must be created given same πᵉ?

**Solution:**
0.05 = 0.03 – 0.4(u – 0.05)
0.02 = –0.4(u – 0.05)
–0.05 = u – 0.05
u = 0 (full employment? Actually u = 0? Let's solve carefully:)

0.05 = 0.03 – 0.4u + 0.02
0.05 = 0.05 – 0.4u
0 = –0.4u ⇒ u = 0 (unrealistic; check: 0.05 = 0.03 – 0.4u + 0.02 = 0.05 – 0.4u ⇒ 0.4u=0 ⇒ u=0)

For u=0.04: π = 0.03 – 0.4(0.04-0.05) = 0.03 – 0.4(–0.01) = 0.03 + 0.004 = 0.034 or 3.4%.

## Problem 5: Debt Dynamics

**Given:** r = 0.05, g = 0.03, initial debt/GDP = 0.8, primary deficit/GDP = 0.02. Find change in debt/GDP ratio.

**Solution:**
Δ(B/Y) = (r – g)(B/Y) + (G–T)/Y
= (0.05–0.03)×0.8 + 0.02
= 0.02×0.8 + 0.02 = 0.016 + 0.02 = 0.036 (3.6 percentage points increase per year)

If this continues, debt/GDP will rise rapidly.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. ❌ Confusing Classical and Keynesian assumptions
   - ✅ Classical: flexible wages/prices, full employment
   - ✅ Keynesian: rigid wages/prices, unemployment possible

2. ❌ Wrong slopes of IS and LM
   - ✅ IS: downward slope
   - ✅ LM: upward slope

3. ❌ Mundell-Fleming results mixed up
   - ✅ Fixed ER: fiscal effective, monetary ineffective
   - ✅ Flexible ER: fiscal ineffective, monetary effective

4. ❌ Money multiplier formula error
   - ✅ m = (cd+1)/(cd+rd)

5. ❌ Phillips Curve: short-run vs long-run
   - ✅ Short-run: downward sloping (trade-off)
   - ✅ Long-run: vertical at natural rate

6. ❌ Forgetting time lags in policy analysis
   - ✅ Mention recognition, implementation, response lags

7. ❌ Neglecting the role of expectations
   - ✅ Adaptive vs. rational expectations change policy conclusions

## Final Tips

- Practice drawing phase diagram for Ramsey model (saddle path)
- Memorize key formulas (multiplier, multiplier-accelerator characteristic equation, debt dynamics)
- Use real-world examples (Great Depression, 2008 crisis, COVID) to illustrate concepts
- For 20-mark questions, always include a diagram description even if not drawn
- In numerical problems, show all steps clearly
